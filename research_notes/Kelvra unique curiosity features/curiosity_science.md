# Curiosity science for Kelvra: what makes 14–24-year-olds "want to know", how curiosity affects memory, and which mechanics are healthy vs manipulative

*Evidence labels:*
- ***Strong*** = several meta-analyses or RCTs agree.
- ***Moderate*** = one meta-analysis or several consistent experiments, with caveats.
- ***Emerging*** = single, small or lab-only studies.
- ***Contested*** = findings conflict.

*Sources marked † are well-established papers cited from the researcher's prior knowledge, with DOI links. They were not re-checked by web search in this session.*

## 1. What makes people (especially 14–24-year-olds) curious? Information-gap theory, newer models, and the inverted U

### Takeaway
Curiosity behaves like a reward-seeking drive for information. It is strongest when a learner notices a specific gap they feel close to closing. That means intermediate confidence (the inverted U), a felt sense of "I almost know this", and expected learning progress. It is driven more by *perceived* knowledge than by actual knowledge.

For the 14–24 age band the baseline is falling. Fifteen-year-olds report less curiosity than 10-year-olds, and intrinsic academic motivation declines across the school grades.

### Cited Findings
- **Information-gap theory.** Curiosity is a form of cognitively induced deprivation. It arises when attention is drawn to a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know.
  - A gap needs a reference point, so curiosity tends to *rise* with domain knowledge: the gap becomes salient and small relative to what is already known.
  - Loewenstein lists four situational triggers: (1) a question, riddle or puzzle; (2) a sequence of events with an anticipated but unknown resolution; (3) violated expectations; (4) knowing that someone else has the information.
  - Source: [Loewenstein 1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1):75–98 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)
- **Inverted U and reward circuitry.** In Kang et al. (2009), 19 undergraduates read trivia questions during fMRI.
  - Curiosity correlated with activity in the caudate, a region linked to anticipated reward.
  - Curiosity was highest at intermediate confidence in one's guess (an inverted U).
  - In behavioural follow-ups, people spent scarce resources (limited tokens or waiting time) to learn answers they were more curious about.
  - Higher curiosity predicted better recall of *surprising* answers 1–2 weeks later.
  - Sources: [Kang et al. 2009, *Psychological Science* 20(8):963–973 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/); [full text (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
- **Perceived knowledge drives curiosity, and the learning boost is small.** In adult trivia experiments, curiosity was best predicted by learners' *own estimate* of their knowledge, more than by an objective measure. Learning was predicted by both curiosity and actual prior knowledge, but there was "only a small boost in learning from being curious". — [Wade & Kidd 2019, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* (PDF)](https://www.kiddlab.com/_files/ugd/0975fd_fe8691bfabc3409b96b14d668cc6bf39.pdf); [summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/knowledge-learning-14070/)
- **Goldilocks attention.** Infants looked away soonest from visual sequences that were too predictable or too surprising, and attended longest at intermediate complexity. — [Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin 2012, *PLOS ONE* †](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0036399)
- **Curiosity as information-seeking.** Kidd & Hayden review curiosity as a form of information-seeking that recruits reward-related neural circuitry and helps motivate learning. They note the field lacks a settled definition. — [Kidd & Hayden 2015, *Neuron* 88(3):449–460 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.010)
- **Learning-progress hypothesis.** Gottlieb, Oudeyer and colleagues model curiosity as active sampling. Learners are intrinsically drawn to activities where their prediction errors are *decreasing fastest*, and they avoid both the already-mastered and the unlearnable. — [Gottlieb, Oudeyer, Lopes & Baranes 2013, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2013.09.001); [Gottlieb & Oudeyer 2018, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* †](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-018-0078-0)
  - Oudeyer and colleagues applied this to educational technology. One example is a bandit-style algorithm (ZPDES) that sequences exercises according to each child's measured learning progress. — [Oudeyer, Gottlieb & Lopes 2016, *Progress in Brain Research* 229 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.pbr.2016.05.005)
- **PACE framework.** The sequence runs prediction error or expectation violation → appraisal → curiosity state → exploration. Curiosity states recruit the dopaminergic circuit, which modulates how the hippocampus encodes and consolidates memories. — [Gruber & Ranganath 2019, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6891259)
- **Knowledge begets curiosity.** Murayama's reward-learning framework proposes that acquiring knowledge is itself rewarding, so successful information-seeking reinforces further seeking. This self-sustaining loop underlies interest development. — [Murayama 2022, *Psychological Review* 129(1):175–198 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000349)
- **Teen decline (age-specific).** OECD's first Survey on Social and Emotional Skills (2019 data, published as *Beyond Academic Learning*, 2021) found lower creativity and curiosity among 15-year-olds than among 10-year-olds. — [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/what-did-oecd-find-in-its-first-ever-survey-on-social-emotional-skills/609197/); [OECD Education Today](https://oecdedutoday.com/new-approach-social-emotional-skills/)
  - OECD's Andreas Schleicher commented: "Our education systems seem to sometimes inhibit the development of social-emotional skills rather than foster it." — [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/what-did-oecd-find-in-its-first-ever-survey-on-social-emotional-skills/609197/)
  - Caveat: surveying 10-year-olds was optional in the 2023 wave, so the age comparison rests mainly on the 2019 data. — [OECD reader's guide](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/social-and-emotional-skills-for-better-lives_35ca7b7c-en/full-report/reader-s-guide_bff3d547.html)
- **Intrinsic motivation falls across grades.** Among 797 US students in grades 3–8, intrinsic motivation declined roughly linearly with grade and correlated positively with grades and test scores. — [Lepper, Corpus & Iyengar 2005, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 97(2) †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.97.2.184)
- **Interventions to raise curiosity exist.** A meta-analysis pooled 41 randomized controlled trials of curiosity-enhancing interventions. The pooled effect was not retrieved (see Gaps). — [Schutte & Malouff 2023, *Current Psychology*](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12144-022-03107-w.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The confidence check doubles as a curiosity sensor.** Kelvra's existing Guess / Unsure / Sure check reads curiosity directly. "Unsure" items sit at the peak of the inverted U. "Sure but wrong" items are where surprise is largest (see section 3). Few study apps use confidence data this way.
- **Show what is already known first.** Curiosity tracks *perceived* closeness to the answer, so a hook should open with what the student already knows ("you have 2 of the 3 pieces") before posing the gap.
- **Recommend by rate of improvement.** The learning-progress model suggests surfacing the topics where a student is improving fastest, not only their weakest or strongest topics.
- **Loewenstein's four triggers map onto app mechanics:**
  - a question → prequestions
  - an unresolved sequence → multi-session arcs
  - a violated expectation → predict-then-reveal
  - someone else knows → peer question walls and the AI mentor
- **Hooks alone will not be enough for this age band.** Curiosity and intrinsic motivation are already declining, so autonomy and relevance levers (sections 6–7) matter as much as hooks.

### Gaps
- I found no India-specific data on how adolescent curiosity changes with age or exam pressure. I could not confirm whether India took part in OECD SSES.
- Inverted-U evidence comes mostly from adult trivia tasks. I found no tests using syllabus content in exam preparation.
- The pooled effect size of Schutte & Malouff (2023) was not retrieved.

## 2. Curiosity and memory (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014; Kang et al. 2009; replications)

### Takeaway
Being curious about an answer reliably helps you remember *that answer*. This shows up in small lab studies and has been extended to 10–14-year-olds.

The famous "spillover" to unrelated material seen during a curious state is fragile. It depends on timing, and a pretesting variant did not reproduce it. No dedicated meta-analysis exists, and in some datasets the curiosity benefit is small.

### Cited Findings
- **The 2014 paradigm.** Each trial ran: trivia question → 14-second wait during which an unrelated face appeared → answer. — [Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014, *Neuron* 84(2):486–496 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/)
  - Memory improved for answers people were curious about. It also improved for the incidental faces seen during high-curiosity states. Both effects held at immediate and one-day-delayed tests.
  - Midbrain and nucleus accumbens activity rose during high curiosity.
  - How much each person's memory for the incidental faces improved tracked their anticipatory midbrain and hippocampal activity, and the connectivity between these regions.
- **Kang et al. (2009).** Higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers 1–2 weeks later. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/)
- **Boundary condition: timing.** Memory for unrelated faces was enhanced only when the faces appeared *shortly after* the question had elicited curiosity. — [Murphy, Dehmelt, Yonelinas, Ranganath & Gruber 2021, *Learning & Memory* 28(2):34](https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/28/2/34.abstract)
- **Failed extension.** In three experiments, guessing before seeing an answer (pretesting) gave a reliable memory benefit for the targets. It gave *no* benefit for incidental material presented before the target. — [*Memory* (2022)](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/09658211.2022.2153141)
- **Adolescents (age-specific).** The study had 60 participants aged 10–14. — [Fandakova & Gruber 2021, *Developmental Science* (Cardiff ORCA)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/132276/); [2019 preprint record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/123423); [Gruber & Fandakova 2021 review (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/mid/EMS129271)
  - Curiosity improved memory for trivia answers in children and adolescents alike.
  - *Interest felt after seeing the answer* boosted memory beyond the effect of curiosity, and more strongly in adolescents than in children.
  - The 2019 preprint called this second effect "surprise"; the published version calls it "interest".
- **Effect size caution.** Once objective prior knowledge is accounted for, there is "only a small boost in learning from being curious." — [Wade & Kidd 2019](https://www.kiddlab.com/_files/ugd/0975fd_fe8691bfabc3409b96b14d668cc6bf39.pdf)
- **No dedicated meta-analysis found.** Searches turned up narrative reviews (e.g., PACE) and one PhD thesis that meta-analysed three of its own magic-trick studies, but no meta-analysis of curiosity and memory. — [Gruber & Ranganath 2019 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6891259); [Melis PhD thesis, University of Reading](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/115600/1/MELISS%20Stefanie_Thesis_amendments.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Build curiosity about the exam content itself.** Do not count on a curiosity "halo" to make dull, unrelated material stick. The incidental-memory effect depends on timing and did not appear with pretesting.
- **Keep the hook-to-reveal gap short.** Seconds, not hours. Put the key fact inside that window.
- **Add a short "why this is interesting" moment right after the reveal.** The Fandakova & Gruber result supports this for 14–17-year-olds. It must stay on topic; irrelevant extras hurt learning (seductive details, section 4).
- **Evidence strength:**
  - **Moderate** for memory of the target answer: consistent findings, but small samples and mostly trivia.
  - **Emerging/Contested** for spillover to incidental material.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis exists.
- Most studies use trivia rather than curriculum, with small samples.
- Long-term retention (weeks to months) of exam content under curiosity manipulations is largely untested.
- I could not verify larger direct replications of the 2014 incidental-face paradigm. Stare et al. (2018), on whether curiosity-driven memory persists over time, was not checked in this session.

## 3. Prediction before reveal: prequestions and pretesting, surprise and prediction error, hypercorrection

### Takeaway
This is the strongest and most app-ready evidence base.
- Answering or guessing *before* studying improves learning of the pre-asked content (meta-analytic g ≈ 0.54).
- The benefit is larger when learners generate an answer (g ≈ 0.65) than when they only read the question (g ≈ 0.22).
- Wrong predictions create surprise, and the size of that surprise tracks learning.
- High-confidence errors spark surprise and curiosity, and they are corrected best (hypercorrection).

Caveats:
- The benefit is mostly confined to the pre-asked information.
- Students dislike strategies that make them err.
- Children with weaker inhibitory control benefit less from wrong predictions.

### Cited Findings
- **Review.** Prequestioning and pretesting "can often enhance learning", even though learners generate many errors on the initial test. — [Pan & Carpenter 2023, *Educational Psychology Review* 35:97](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)
  - The effect has been shown with text, video and lectures.
  - The mechanisms, which the authors organise into a three-stage framework, appear to involve test-induced changes in later learning behaviour.
  - Moderators and best classroom practice still need research.
- **Meta-analysis.** A preregistered meta-analysis found a moderate effect on prequestioned material (g = 0.54; reported as k = 97). The effect on *non*-prequestioned material was virtually absent. — [St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 31:411–441](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8)
  - A later review reports a larger effect when learners had to generate answers (g = 0.65) than when they only read the prequestions (g = 0.22). — [Rivers et al. 2026 review (PDF)](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/RBPT_2026.pdf)
- **Student resistance and delay (secondary source).** Students tend to resist strategies that make them commit errors. One summary reports the largest benefits at a 1-week delay. — [Ollie Lovell, summary of Pan & Carpenter](https://www.ollielovell.com/how-you-can-use-prequestioning-to-boost-student-learning/)
- **Prediction → surprise → learning.** — [Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge 2018, *Learning and Instruction* 55:22–31 (record)](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100090302758_0x000001); [press release](https://idw-online.de/en/news693157)
  - Predicting an answer before seeing it improved memory for it, especially when the prediction was false.
  - Unexpected outcomes produced a surprise response (pupil dilation) only when a prediction had been made. The strength of that response was positively related to learning.
  - The authors caution that this does not prove surprise is the cause.
- **Explicit predictions raise the stakes.** Making an explicit prediction increases the learner's stake in the outcome, which especially benefits learning of unexpected outcomes. — [Brod et al. 2022, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1)
- **Prediction stimulates curiosity** by making the knowledge gap more relevant to the learner. — [Brod & Breitwieser 2019, *npj Science of Learning* (PDF)](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2022/25224/pdf/NPJ_2019_4_Brod_et_al_Lighting_the_wick_in_the_candle_of_learning_A.pdf)
- **Age boundary.** Among 9–12-year-olds, only those with better inhibitory control learned from incorrectly predicted outcomes. — [Brod et al. 2020, *Developmental Science*](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/desc.12916)
- **Hypercorrection.** Errors made with high confidence are *more* likely to be corrected after feedback than low-confidence errors. — [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 27(6) †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491)
  - Metcalfe's review argues that errors followed by corrective feedback enhance learning, which challenges the "errorless learning" view. — [Metcalfe 2017, *Annual Review of Psychology* 68 †](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022)
- **Epistemic emotions.** Three trivia studies with immediate feedback (Ns = 102, 373, 125) looked at surprise, curiosity and confusion. — [Vogl, Pekrun, Murayama & Loderer 2020, *Emotion*](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8q8zv/surprised-curious-confused-epistemic-emotions-and-knowledge-exploration); [Vogl et al. 2019 replication, *Frontiers in Psychology* (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6861443)
  - All three emotions were most strongly tied to high-confidence errors.
  - Surprise and curiosity positively predicted later knowledge exploration. Confusion did so less consistently: its effects were significant in Studies 1 and 3 but not in Study 2.
  - Unlike these epistemic emotions, achievement emotions such as pride and shame did not have positive effects on exploration.
- **Struggle before instruction.** A meta-analysis of "productive failure" (problem-solving before instruction) found an advantage for conceptual knowledge over instruction-first teaching (g ≈ 0.36 across 53 studies). The advantage was larger when designs followed the approach faithfully. — [Sinha & Kapur 2021, *Review of Educational Research* 91(5) †](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- **AI tutor evidence.** A field experiment gave nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school maths students (Fall 2023) one of two GPT-4 tutors. — [Bastani et al. 2025, *PNAS* 122(26):e2422633122 (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [SSRN version](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486); [Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)
  - Students used an unrestricted ChatGPT-style interface ("GPT Base") as a "crutch" and later performed worse on their own.
  - A tutor prompted to give hints rather than answers ("GPT Tutor") largely removed this harm.
  - Per the SSRN abstract †: practice performance rose 48% (Base) and 127% (Tutor). Once access was removed, Base users scored 17% lower than controls.
  - AI users were also overly optimistic about how much they had learned.

### Inferences
- **The confidence check can become a hypercorrection engine.** Kelvra already collects Guess / Unsure / Sure. Paired with *immediate* feedback, "Sure + wrong" should trigger a distinct, memorable reveal and an early retest.
- **Make predictions active.** The student should tap an option, type a number or drag a slider. Merely reading a question is the weaker condition (g ≈ 0.22).
- **Spend prequestions on the highest-yield content.** They mostly help the pre-asked material, so target the most important exam concepts (e.g., by past-paper frequency).
- **Make guessing explicitly free.** No marks lost, no streak at stake, and a one-time message that "wrong guesses help you learn". This counters error aversion, which is probably amplified for Indian students under rank pressure (an untested inference).
- **Scaffold after surprises.** For younger or struggling users, follow a surprise with a short, structured explanation (Brod et al. 2020).
- **Keep the gap open in the AI mentor.** It should default to hint-first, Socratic help rather than closing the information gap instantly.

### Gaps
- Most prequestion studies use text, video or lectures in labs and classrooms. I found none run inside mobile flashcard or spaced-repetition apps, or with Indian exam-prep content.
- It is untested whether "predict first" loses potency when applied to every card (habituation).
- The claim that benefits are largest at a 1-week delay comes from a secondary blog and was not checked against the primary review.

## 4. Mystery, narrative and story hooks; questions-first teaching; curiosity-gap headlines and the clickbait backfire

### Takeaway
Narrative or mystery framing can trigger situational interest, but the evidence that it improves learning is weaker than for prequestions. Three findings set the limits:
- Interesting-but-irrelevant content measurably *hurts* learning (seductive details, g = −0.33).
- Unfinished tasks are reliably *resumed*, but not better *remembered*.
- Clickbait-style curiosity gaps cut credibility, most of all for unfamiliar publishers.

So the hook should be the syllabus content itself (a real question or paradox), and it must pay off immediately.

### Cited Findings
- **Seductive details.** A meta-analysis of 68 effects from 58 studies found that interesting but irrelevant additions reduced learning (g = −0.33). — [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:707–734 (ScienceDaily)](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200319103223.htm); [ACU summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details); [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Seductive%20Details%20in%20Training%20041821.pdf)
  - The harm was larger when learners could not control the pacing, when such details were frequent or in the same format as the core content, and for novices.
  - Effects were considerable in short lessons and not significant in longer ones.
  - One summary reports *no* moderator differences, which conflicts with the above, so treat the moderators cautiously.
- **Narrative games.** Adding a narrative theme to a science learning game did not improve learning compared with a plain slideshow. Students learned less and took longer. — [Adams, Mayer, MacNamara, Koenig & Wainess 2012, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 104(1) †](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025595)
- **Game fiction in gamification.** Including "game fiction" (narrative framing) significantly moderated *behavioural* outcomes. It was not shown to drive cognitive outcomes. — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- **Cliffhangers: Zeigarnik vs Ovsiankina.** A 2025 meta-analysis found no reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks (the Zeigarnik effect). It did find a general tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect). — [Ghibellini & Meier 2025, *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications* 12](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
- **Learner-generated questions.** Across 26 intervention studies, teaching students to generate their own questions improved comprehension. Median effect sizes were 0.36 on standardised tests and 0.86 on experimenter-made tests. — [Rosenshine, Meister & Chapman 1996, *Review of Educational Research* 66(2) †](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543066002181)
- **AI-trained question-asking.** In 75 primary-school children aged 9–10, GPT-3-generated cues trained "curious" question-asking as well as hand-written cues did. *Open* GPT-3 cues outperformed closed ones. — [Abdelghani et al. 2023, *International Journal of AI in Education* (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14228)
- **Clickbait and trust (includes Indian participants).** IIIT Hyderabad researchers showed 200 participants from North America and India real news articles with either clickbait or non-clickbait headlines. — [IIIT Hyderabad research news](https://blogs.iiit.ac.in/monthly_news/dr-kavita-vemuri-and-vivek-kaushals-paper-in-early-access-area-on-ieee-xplore/); [IIIT, "The importance of an honest headline"](https://iiit.ac.in/?p=18460)
  - Clickbait significantly lowered credibility ratings but produced no significant difference in clicks.
  - Specific epistemic curiosity was weakly *negatively* correlated with a tendency to click clickbait.
- **Clickbait hits unfamiliar publishers hardest.** In a preregistered experiment (main n ≈ 5,040), clickbait ads embedded in news articles substantially reduced the articles' perceived credibility. The effect was driven by the *least well-known publishers*. — [Native advertising and the credibility of online publishers (AMiner record)](https://aminer.org/pub/64a3f363d68f896efac0c197/native-advertising-and-the-credibility-of-online-publishers)
- **Scepticism spills over.** Australian research (2026) found audiences flag clickbait-style headlines as misleading, and warned that "scepticism can spill over into disengagement from credible news." — [AdNews](https://www.adnews.com.au/news/trust-in-news-is-being-eroded-by-clickbait-headlines)

### Inferences
- **Content-native mysteries.** The hook should be a real paradox or exam question from the student's own notes ("why doesn't…?"), not unrelated trivia.
- **Extras come after the reveal.** "Whoa" facts should be optional, one tap away and paced by the learner. Pacing control reduces the seductive-details penalty.
- **Use cliffhangers to bring students back, not to claim memory gains.**
  - Ending a session on an open question will likely bring students back (Ovsiankina), but it should not be claimed to improve memory.
  - Open the next session with that same question as a prequestion, which adds the prequestion benefit.
- **Notifications must pay off on the first screen.** A new brand like Kelvra is exactly the "less well-known publisher" whose credibility suffers most from clickbait.
- **Train question-asking with open-ended AI prompts.** This rests on the evidence that learner-generated questions improve comprehension.

### Gaps
- There is little controlled evidence on mystery or detective framing of curriculum content for teens. The Adams et al. (2012) narrative-game findings were cited from prior knowledge (†).
- The Abdelghani study involved 9–10-year-olds. I found no equivalent for 14–24-year-olds.
- I found no evidence on curiosity-gap notification copy in edtech specifically.

## 5. Variable rewards, novelty and gamification: which elements help learning and which do not

### Takeaway
Dopamine neurons signal reward prediction errors, and sustained dopamine activity peaks at maximal reward uncertainty (p = 0.5). This is why variable rewards grab attention. In learning games, uncertain rewards attracted engagement in small studies, but I found no rigorous classroom learning results.

Gamification meta-analyses show small-to-medium positive effects:
- cognitive outcomes: g ≈ 0.46–0.50
- motivational outcomes: g ≈ 0.36
- behavioural outcomes: g ≈ 0.25

The effects depend on which elements are used and fade as novelty wears off. Points and leaderboards raise *output* without raising intrinsic motivation, and can backfire.

### Cited Findings
- **Reward prediction error.** Dopamine neurons encode the difference between the reward received and the reward predicted. — [Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997, *Science* 275 †](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.275.5306.1593)
  - Sustained dopamine activity was maximal when reward probability was 0.5, i.e., at maximal uncertainty. — [Fiorillo, Tobler & Schultz 2003, *Science* 299 †](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1077349)
- **Uncertain rewards in learning games.** — [Howard-Jones et al., "Toward a science of learning games" (PDF)](https://www.mrcbndu.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_files/Toward%20a%20Science%20of%20Learning%20Games.pdf); [TES](https://www.tes.com/news/staking-students-learning-spin-wheel); [Karl Kapp summary](https://karlkapp.com/?p=4799)
  - In a quiz game (Howard-Jones & Demetriou 2009, *Instructional Science*), learners preferred the uncertain-reward option, and the preference *grew* during the quiz. The authors argue "gaming uncertainty" may improve engagement and possibly memory encoding.
  - A Bristol/Wellcome classroom trial compared three arms: games with uncertain rewards, tests with fixed rewards, and usual teaching. It ran to December 2017. I could not find its results.
  - Press coverage of a separate 24-student study overstated its findings.
- **Motivating-uncertainty effect.** While pursuing a reward, people put in more effort for an uncertain reward (e.g., $1 or $2) than for a certain one, even though they prefer certain rewards when choosing in advance. — [Shen, Fishbach & Hsee 2015, *Journal of Consumer Research* 41(5) †](https://doi.org/10.1086/679418)
- **The Hook model.** Eyal's habit loop is trigger → action → *variable reward* → investment. — [Eyal 2014, *Hooked* †](https://books.google.com/books?vid=ISBN9781591847786)
  - His own "Manipulation Matrix" counts a habit-forming product as ethical (a "facilitator") only if the maker would use it and it materially improves users' lives.
- **Gamification meta-analysis 1.** — [Sailer & Homner 2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:77–112](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [author summary](https://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)

  | Outcome | g | k | N |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Cognitive | 0.49 | 19 | 1,686 |
  | Motivational | 0.36 | 16 | 2,246 |
  | Behavioural | 0.25 | 9 | 951 |

  - The cognitive effect held up in a subset of high-rigour studies. Motivational and behavioural effects were less stable.
  - Game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes. Competition combined with collaboration also looked beneficial.
- **Gamification meta-analysis 2.** g = 0.504 in favour of gamification, across 30 independent interventions from 24 studies (3,202 participants). Sources cite the journal volume as either 30 or 33. — [Bai, Hew & Huang 2020, *Educational Research Review* (UZH reference)](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020)); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2020.100322)
- **Gamification meta-analysis 3.** g = 0.464. It examined 14 design elements (including badges and leaderboards), each with different effects. — [Huang et al. 2020, *Educational Technology Research & Development* 68](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11423-020-09807-z)
- **Which elements serve which needs.** In an experiment with 419 participants: — [Sailer, Hense, Mayr & Mandl 2017, *Computers in Human Behavior* 69 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033)
  - Badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised the sense of competence and perceived task meaningfulness.
  - Avatars, meaningful stories and teammates raised the sense of social relatedness.
  - No element raised autonomy.
- **More output, not more motivation.** Points, levels and leaderboards increased task output (n = 273) but not intrinsic motivation or competence satisfaction. — [Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch & Opwis 2017, *Computers in Human Behavior* 71 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048)
- **Backfire.** In a 16-week college course, students in the gamified section (badges, leaderboard, competition) showed declining intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment. They also scored lower on the final exam than the non-gamified section. — [Hanus & Fox 2015, *Computers & Education* 80 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019)
- **Novelty effect.** A 14-week quasi-experiment in a Brazilian introductory programming course measured outcomes at seven time points. — [Rodrigues et al. 2022, *International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education* 19](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6); [summary](https://www.universityxp.com/research/2022/10/3/gamification-suffers-from-the-novelty-effect-but-benefits-from-the-familiarization-effect-findings-from-a-longitudinal-study)
  - Gamification's impact followed a U-shape: it fell as novelty wore off.
  - It then partly recovered between weeks 6 and 10, which the authors call a "familiarization effect".

### Inferences
- **Vary the content, not the value.**
  - Uncertainty about *what you will discover* (the next fact, the twist) is curiosity.
  - Uncertainty about *how much you will win* (points, coins, prizes) is the slot-machine lever, and it drifts toward loot-box territory (section 8).
- **Favour elements that carry competence information.** Progress graphs and mastery maps, plus collaboration, are better than leaderboards and competition, especially for anxious exam-takers.
- **Plan for novelty decay.** Judge features at 6–10+ weeks, not in the first fortnight, and rotate formats.
- **Attach game elements to retrieval and prediction, not to time-on-app.** Gamification's effect on cognitive outcomes is the most robust one, and retrieval and prediction are what drive learning.

### Gaps
- I could not find the outcome of the Bristol/Wellcome uncertain-rewards classroom trial.
- Element-level results from Bai et al. (2020) were not retrieved.
- I found no gamification studies with Indian exam-prep students.
- Several element-level studies (Sailer et al. 2017; Mekler et al. 2017; Hanus & Fox 2015) are cited from prior knowledge (†).

## 6. Self-determination theory, intrinsic motivation and the overjustification effect

### Takeaway
Autonomy, competence and relatedness sustain engagement. A 128-study meta-analysis found that *expected, tangible, task-contingent* rewards undermine later free-choice interest, and more so for children. Informational positive feedback, by contrast, *enhances* interest (d ≈ +0.33).

Critics (Cameron and Pierce) contest how general the undermining effect is. But they agree that harm occurs when rewards are tangible, expected and loosely tied to performance, which describes most typical app rewards.

### Cited Findings
- **Self-determination theory (SDT).** Intrinsic motivation and internalisation are supported when the needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness are met. Controlling contexts thwart them. — [Ryan & Deci 2000, *American Psychologist* 55(1) †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68)
- **Overjustification meta-analysis (128 studies).** — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6):627–668 (PDF)](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)
  - Rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation whether they were contingent on engagement (d = −0.40), completion (d = −0.36) or performance (d = −0.28).
  - All rewards, all tangible rewards and all expected rewards also undermined it.
  - Engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also lowered self-reported interest.
  - Positive feedback *enhanced* free-choice behaviour (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31).
  - The abstract also states that tangible rewards were more detrimental, and verbal rewards less enhancing, for children than for college students † (from prior knowledge of the abstract; the subgroup values were not re-verified).
- **Contested.** Cameron and colleagues argue rewards are not harmful in general. They find negative effects only when rewards are tangible, expected (offered beforehand) and loosely tied to performance. — [Cameron, Banko & Pierce 2001, *The Behavior Analyst* 24](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1007/bf03392017)
- **The classic demonstration.** Preschoolers who *expected* a reward for drawing later spent less free time drawing than children who got no reward or an unexpected one. — [Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 28(1) †](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519)
- **Neural evidence.** Paying for performance on a task reduced later voluntary engagement once the payment stopped (n = 28). Task-related activity in the anterior striatum and prefrontal cortex also dropped after payment ended. — [Murayama et al. 2010, *PNAS* 107(49) †](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1013305107)
- **Element-level evidence (see section 5).**
  - Points, levels and leaderboards raise output, not intrinsic motivation. — [Mekler et al. 2017 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048)
  - No common game element raised autonomy. — [Sailer et al. 2017 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033)

### Inferences
- **Make rewards informational and unexpected.** Say "you can now explain X" or "your error rate on Y halved". Avoid pre-announced prizes for time spent or cards completed.
- **Never turn study into currency.** No coins or badges-for-minutes for 14–17-year-olds, since children show stronger undermining.
- **Add real choice.** Autonomy is the need gamification fails to serve (Sailer et al. 2017). Let students choose which mystery to open, which path to take, and whether features like streaks are on at all.
- **Build relatedness without ranking.** Use the mentor relationship, study-buddy collaboration and anonymous peer questions. Avoid public leaderboards.

### Gaps
- The overall tangible-reward d (often quoted as −0.34) and the child vs college subgroup values were not verified, because the PDF host was blocked.
- I did not search for SDT studies in Indian high-stakes exam contexts or for cross-cultural evidence on autonomy support.

## 7. Interest development (Hidi & Renninger 2006) and relevance interventions (Hulleman & Harackiewicz 2009)

### Takeaway
Interest develops in four phases: triggered situational → maintained situational → emerging individual → well-developed individual. Hooks only start this process; *meaning and relevance* sustain it.
- Brief, self-written relevance essays raised interest and grades for high-school students who expected to do poorly.
- Motivation interventions average d ≈ 0.49.
- Personalising problems to students' real interests raised situational interest and learning.

### Cited Findings
- **Four-phase model.** — [Hidi & Renninger 2006, *Educational Psychologist* 41(2):111–127 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4)
  - Triggered situational interest often comes from novelty, surprise or personal relevance.
  - It can become maintained situational interest through meaningful tasks and personal involvement.
  - From there it can grow into emerging and then well-developed individual interest. These later phases are marked by stored knowledge, stored value, and the learner generating their own curiosity questions.
  - Earlier phases need more external support.
- **Utility-value RCT.** — [Hulleman & Harackiewicz 2009, *Science* 326:1410–1412 (PDF)](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/hulleman_harackiewicz_2009_-_promoting_interest_performance_in_high_school_science_classes.pdf); [review citing the grade effect (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5589190)
  - 262 ninth-graders taught by seven science teachers were randomly assigned either to write about how course material applied to their lives or to write summaries.
  - The relevance writing raised interest in science and course grades for students with *low* performance expectations.
  - A later review puts the grade gain at about two-thirds of a letter grade. This figure was not verified against the paper.
- **College replication.** In college biology, a utility-value writing intervention reduced the achievement gap for first-generation, underrepresented-minority students by 61%. — [Harackiewicz et al. 2016, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 111(5) †](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000075)
- **Meta-analysis of motivation interventions.** Across 74 papers (92 effect sizes, 38,377 participants), d = 0.49 (95% CI ≈ 0.43–0.56; another source gives 0.42–0.56). Randomised designs showed smaller effects than quasi-experiments. — [Lazowski & Hulleman 2016, *Review of Educational Research* 86 (CORE record)](https://core.ac.uk/works/50039030); [MSPnet](https://www.mspnet.org/library/29377.html)
- **Context personalisation.** A year-long study of 141 ninth-graders gave algebra problems tailored to students' out-of-school interests. — [SMU Research blog](https://blog.smu.edu/research/?p=6799); [EdWeek](https://edweek.org/teaching-learning/studies-find-payoff-in-personalizing-algebra/2012/09); [KQED MindShift](https://ww2.kqed.org/mindshift/32768/to-learn-algebra-the-not-so-secret-way-to-students-hearts)
  - Students mastered the concepts faster.
  - They later did better on harder problems, even in a unit *without* personalisation.
  - Months later they reported greater interest in maths and scored higher on a classroom test.
  - Walkington stresses that authentic connections (how students actually use numbers in their interests) matter more than inserting a favourite food or a pet's name.
- **Why personalisation works.** It led high-school students to report more *triggered situational interest*, which in turn predicted their learning outcomes in the tutoring software. — [Bernacki & Walkington 2018, *Journal of Educational Psychology* (UNLV record)](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/edpsych_fac_articles/190)
- **Post-answer interest** boosts memory more in adolescents than in children (see section 2). — [Fandakova & Gruber 2021](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/132276/)

### Inferences
- **Use the Career/Project Explorer for relevance prompts.** Link a chapter to the student's chosen career and ask *them* to write the connection in 1–2 sentences. Writing it themselves is what works; being told is weaker. Target low-confidence students first.
- **Personalise examples with the AI.** An LLM can rewrite examples into each student's stated interests (cricket, music, coding, farming, local context). Authenticity matters, so students should pick their own interests and the AI should use realistic numbers.
- **Move users through the four phases:**
  - Hooks trigger situational interest.
  - Series, arcs and personal relevance maintain it.
  - Student-authored questions and projects support emerging individual interest.

### Gaps
- I could not verify large-scale or online replications of utility-value interventions; some are reported to show null effects.
- The exact grade effect in Hulleman & Harackiewicz (2009) was not checked against the paper.
- I found no relevance-intervention studies in Indian schools or coaching contexts.

## 8. Dark patterns and ethics: where curiosity mechanics become manipulative, and risks specific to minors (UK, EU, India)

### Takeaway
The same levers that build curiosity become manipulative when they do any of the following:
- monetise uncertainty (loot-box-style randomness)
- use loss or fear of missing out (FOMO) to compel return (streaks, artificially timed notifications)
- remove stopping cues (infinite scroll, autoplay)
- mislead (clickbait, false urgency, confirm-shaming)
- exploit parents' fears

Regulators now treat several of these as unacceptable for under-18s:
- the UK ICO Children's Code
- the EU Digital Services Act (DSA) guidelines on minors (July 2025)
- India's CCPA Dark Patterns Guidelines (2023)
- India's Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act 2023, which bars behavioural monitoring of under-18s

### Cited Findings
- **How common dark patterns are.** A crawl of about 53K product pages on about 11K shopping sites found 1,818 dark-pattern instances (15 types in 7 categories) on 1,254 sites (~11.1%). That included 183 sites with outright deceptive patterns. — [Mathur et al. 2019, *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction (CSCW)* †](https://doi.org/10.1145/3359183)
- **Practitioner taxonomy:** nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, forced action. — [Gray et al. 2018, CHI †](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174108)
- **Lenses for judging how "dark" a pattern is:** individual welfare, collective welfare, regulatory objectives, autonomy. — [Mathur, Kshirsagar & Mayer 2021, CHI †](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445610)
- **UK ICO Children's Code, Standard 13 (Nudge techniques).** — [ICO Standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/ico-codes-of-practice/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques); [Safer Internet UK summary](https://saferinternet.org.uk/blog/icos-age-appropriate-design-code)
  - Do not use nudges to lead or encourage children to give more personal data, turn off privacy protections, or misstate their age.
  - Nudges toward wellbeing *are* encouraged. The ICO's examples are suggesting breaks and providing pause-and-save features.
- **EU DSA Article 28 guidelines (14 July 2025).** They are voluntary, but the Commission uses them as a compliance benchmark. — [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors); [Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/07/rd-european-commission-guidelines-on-protection-of-minors-under-the-digital-services-act)
  - Features that drive excessive use should be off by default: communication streaks, ephemeral content, read receipts, autoplay and push notifications.
  - Minors should not be exposed to persuasive design aimed mainly at engagement, "such as the possibility to scroll indefinitely, notifications artificially timed to regain minors' attention, virtual rewards".
  - Persuasive design in AI chatbots should be critically reviewed.
- **Loot boxes and adolescents.**
  - In 1,155 16–18-year-olds, loot-box spending was linked to problem gambling with "moderate to large" magnitude, stronger than in adults. Purchase motives resembled gambling motives. The study is correlational. — [Zendle, Meyer & Over 2019, *Royal Society Open Science* (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599795)
  - A meta-analysis found r = 0.26 between loot-box spending and problem gambling (0.37 after trim-and-fill correction; 15 studies), and r = 0.25 with excessive gaming (7 studies). — [Garea et al. 2021, *International Gambling Studies* 21(3)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:intgms:v:21:y:2021:i:3:p:460-479)
  - A separate meta-analysis of 13 studies found r ≈ 0.27. All the underlying studies are cross-sectional. — [Spicer et al., summarised in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9295209/)
- **Streaks and teens.** In 2,483 Belgian early adolescents (mean age about 13.5), keeping Snapchat streaks was associated with problematic smartphone use. — [van Essen & Van Ouytsel 2023, *Telematics and Informatics Reports* (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/7e7e45c958c74b29ba203607b6f4a840)
  - Correlations with FOMO and low social-media self-control were weak.
  - Girls were more likely to keep streaks, and kept them longer.
- **FOMO.** Fear of missing out was higher in younger people. It was linked to lower satisfaction of basic psychological needs and to greater social-media engagement. — [Przybylski et al. 2013, *Computers in Human Behavior* 29(4) †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2013.02.014)
- **Teen sensitivity to social reward.** In 32 adolescents aged 13–18, seeing their own photos with many "likes" activated the nucleus accumbens (reward circuitry). Teens were also more likely to "like" photos that already had many likes. — [Sherman et al. 2016, *Psychological Science* 27(7) †](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616645673)
- **India: CCPA Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023.** Notified 30 Nov 2023 under section 18 of the Consumer Protection Act 2019. — [JSA Law](https://www.jsalaw.com/newsletters-and-updates/ccpa-issues-guidelines-for-prevention-and-regulation-of-dark-patterns-2023/); [Mondaq overview](https://www.mondaq.com/article/1844140/ccpas-guidelines-on-dark-patterns-an-overview)
  - They apply to platforms offering goods or services in India, and to advertisers and sellers.
  - Annexure 1 lists 13 specified practices: false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing, and rogue malware.
  - In June 2025 the CCPA advised e-commerce platforms to self-audit within three months. It has since applied the Guidelines in enforcement proceedings.
- **India: DPDP Act 2023, Section 9.** — [DPDPA s.9 text](https://dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section9.html); [Bar & Bench analysis of the draft Rules](https://www.barandbench.com/law-firms/view-point/childs-personal-data-and-privacy-analysing-the-draft-dpdp-rules-2025); [S.S. Rana on schools, coaching centres and platforms](https://ssrana.in/articles/schools-coaching-centres-platforms-processing-childrens-data-are-you-compliant/)
  - A "child" is anyone under 18, and verifiable parental consent is required.
  - Section 9(3): "A Data Fiduciary shall not undertake tracking or behavioural monitoring of children or targeted advertising directed at children."
  - Processing likely to harm a child's well-being is also barred.
  - The draft DPDP Rules (Jan 2025) set out verifiable-consent mechanisms (Rule 10). Schedule 4 lets *educational institutions* track or monitor children only for their safety and for the institution's education activities.
- **India: fear-based selling to parents.** In December 2022 India's child-rights commission (NCPCR) summoned BYJU'S CEO over alleged hard-selling. BYJU'S denied the allegations. — [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/byjus-buying-phone-numbers-of-kids-threatening-parents-ncpcr-chief/1274281); [Context](https://www.context.news/socioeconomic-inclusion/indian-tech-firm-byjus-hauled-in-by-watchdog-over-context-expose); [Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/india/child-rights-commission-issues-summons-to-byjus-ceo-over-hard-selling-of-courses)
  - The NCPCR chair alleged that the company bought phone numbers of children and parents.
  - He alleged it targeted first-generation learners.
  - He alleged it told parents their children's "future would be ruined".

### Inferences
- **Red lines for Kelvra:**
  - No paid or currency-linked randomised rewards.
  - No loss-framed streaks for minors. Use a forgiving weekly rhythm instead, with streaks opt-in at most.
  - No notifications timed by inactivity to "win back" minors, and no late-night pushes.
  - No infinite content feeds.
  - In upsells, no fake urgency (e.g., "only 2 seats left at this price"), no confirm-shaming (e.g., "No, I don't care about my rank") and no nagging. These map onto the CCPA's 13 patterns.
  - No public like counts or rank leaderboards for under-18s.
- **Keep the Family Update informational, never fearful.**
  - Report effort and learning in neutral language.
  - The student should be able to see, and ideally co-control, what is shared.
  - It must never become a fear channel to parents (the BYJU'S precedent).
- **Get legal review on DPDP before launch.**
  - For 14–17-year-olds, using behavioural data to personalise *engagement* nudges may count as "behavioural monitoring".
  - Even personalisation for *learning* (spaced repetition, mastery tracking) needs review. The education carve-out names "educational institutions", and an app may not qualify.
- **Ethics test for each feature:**
  - Would the student endorse the mechanic if they fully understood it? (autonomy lens)
  - Does it improve learning or wellbeing, not just minutes in the app? (individual-welfare lens)
  - Would the team use it themselves? (Eyal's "facilitator" test)
  - Does it pass the CCPA, ICO and DSA lists?
- **Pro-wellbeing nudges are explicitly endorsed.** Break reminders, pause-and-save and links to support all fit Kelvra's focus timer and stress reset.

### Gaps
- The full texts of ICO Standard 5 ("Detrimental use of data") and Standard 13 could not be fetched because ico.org.uk was blocked. Their wording comes from search summaries.
- The final notified DPDP Rules 2025 text was not verified; only draft-stage commentary was found. Check whether the educational carve-out covers edtech apps.
- I found no peer-reviewed studies of Duolingo-style *learning* streaks and anxiety. The Snapchat-streak evidence is correlational, with small effects.
- I found no India-specific research on dark patterns in edtech beyond the NCPCR case and the CCPA guidelines.

## 9. Synthesis: 15 concrete, evidence-based curiosity mechanics for Kelvra (evidence strength and in-app example)

### Takeaway
Kelvra's best-evidenced and most distinctive bets are:
1. **Prediction-first reveals plus "plot-twist" replays of high-confidence errors.** These build on the existing Guess / Unsure / Sure check and rest on prequestion and hypercorrection evidence (strong to moderate).
2. **An AI mentor that keeps the gap open,** using hint ladders and question-asking coaching. This rests on one large field experiment plus meta-analyses on struggle-first learning and question generation.
3. **Relevance and personalisation through the Career Explorer,** which rests on randomised trials plus a meta-analysis.

Avoid:
- variable *value* rewards (variable *content* surprises are fine)
- loss-framed streaks
- leaderboards
- clickbait pushes
- fear-based messages to parents

### Cited Findings
Each mechanic lists what it is, its evidence strength, a one-line example in Kelvra, and sources.

1. **Bet-first reveal ("predict before you peek").**
   - *What:* One quick, active prediction before a summary section, flashcard back or video segment opens. No marks lost for wrong guesses.
   - *Evidence:* **Strong** for the pre-asked content (meta-analytic g = 0.54; generating g = 0.65 vs reading g = 0.22). **Moderate** for surprise as the mechanism.
   - *Example:* Before the Electrostatics summary: "Guess: double the distance between two charges — the force becomes ½, ¼ or 2×?" → tap → "You said ½. It's ¼ (inverse-square). 20-second why →"
   - *Sources:* [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8); [Rivers et al. 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/RBPT_2026.pdf); [Brod et al. 2018](https://idw-online.de/en/news693157); [Pan & Carpenter 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)

2. **Plot-twist replay (hypercorrection engine).**
   - *What:* "Sure + wrong" answers from the confidence check get a distinct reveal. They return later in the same session and again the next day. "Guess + right" is flagged as a lucky guess to re-check. The Test Debrief opens with the student's top 3 "plot twists".
   - *Evidence:* **Moderate–Strong.** Hypercorrection is a robust lab effect, and three studies link high-confidence errors to surprise, curiosity and exploration.
   - *Example:* "Plot twist! You were SURE the Battle of Plassey was 1764. It was 1757 (1764 = Buxar). Want the 2-line memory hook?"
   - *Sources:* [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491); [Metcalfe 2017 †](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022); [Vogl et al. 2020](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8q8zv/surprised-curious-confused-epistemic-emotions-and-knowledge-exploration); [Vogl et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6861443)

3. **Calibrated "Mystery of the Day".**
   - *What:* One real puzzle from tomorrow's chapter, chosen from the student's "Unsure" zone (intermediate confidence) and framed by what they already know. The answer is always delivered within the session, never paywalled or withheld.
   - *Evidence:* **Moderate.** Supported by the inverted U and by curiosity improving memory for targets, but effects are small and mostly from trivia studies.
   - *Example:* "Why does adding salt lower the freezing point of water? You already know 2 of the 3 ideas from your Solutions notes — find the third in today's 6-minute kit."
   - *Sources:* [Kang et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/); [Wade & Kidd 2019](https://www.kiddlab.com/_files/ugd/0975fd_fe8691bfabc3409b96b14d668cc6bf39.pdf); [Loewenstein 1994 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)

4. **Frontier map (learning-progress recommender).**
   - *What:* A visual topic map that highlights where the student's accuracy is *rising fastest* (not their weakest or mastered topics). The student chooses among 2–3 "frontier" options, which gives them autonomy.
   - *Evidence:* **Emerging.** Strong theory and computational work, but few classroom tests.
   - *Example:* "Your frontier this week: Electrochemistry (55%, climbing fastest). 3 cards could tip it to 'mastered'. Or explore: Coordination Compounds / Thermodynamics."
   - *Sources:* [Gottlieb & Oudeyer 2018 †](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-018-0078-0); [Oudeyer et al. 2016 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.pbr.2016.05.005); [Sailer et al. 2017 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033)

5. **Hint-ladder AI mentor (keep the gap open).**
   - *What:* By default the mentor replies with a question or hint, then escalates: nudge → method → worked step → full solution on request. Before each step up, the student predicts the next step.
   - *Evidence:* **Moderate–Strong.** One large field experiment on AI tutors, plus a productive-failure meta-analysis for conceptual knowledge.
   - *Example:* "Solve ∫x·eˣ dx" → Mentor: "Which factor gets simpler when you differentiate it? (Hint 1 of 3)"
   - *Sources:* [Bastani et al. 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [Sinha & Kapur 2021 †](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)

6. **Ask-it-better question coach.**
   - *What:* After reading notes, the student writes the question an examiner (or a curious friend) would ask. The AI gives *open* cues to deepen it (what → why → what if). The best student questions become their own flashcards. This fits naturally with the Answer-Writing Coach.
   - *Evidence:* **Moderate.** A review of 26 question-generation studies found median effect sizes of 0.36 on standardised tests and 0.86 on researcher-made tests. A controlled study with AI-generated cues found gains in 9–10-year-olds.
   - *Example:* "Your question: 'What is osmosis?' Good start — can you turn it into a 'why' question an examiner would give 5 marks for?"
   - *Sources:* [Rosenshine et al. 1996 †](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543066002181); [Abdelghani et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14228)

7. **"Why would I care?" relevance prompt (utility-value writing).**
   - *What:* Occasionally (about once per chapter, not per card), the student writes 1–2 sentences linking the topic to their life or to the career they chose in the Career Explorer. On by default for low-confidence students.
   - *Evidence:* **Moderate–Strong.** Randomised trials in high school and college, and a motivation-intervention meta-analysis (d ≈ 0.49). Strongest for students who expect to do poorly. Some null results at scale are reported but were not verified.
   - *Example:* "You picked 'Civil Engineer'. In two lines: where would Hooke's law matter when designing a bridge?"
   - *Sources:* [Hulleman & Harackiewicz 2009](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/hulleman_harackiewicz_2009_-_promoting_interest_performance_in_high_school_science_classes.pdf); [Harackiewicz et al. 2016 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000075); [Lazowski & Hulleman 2016](https://core.ac.uk/works/50039030)

8. **Your-world examples (AI context personalisation).**
   - *What:* The student picks 2–3 real interests (cricket, music, gaming, farming, coding, cooking). The AI rewrites practice problems and examples into those contexts with realistic numbers.
   - *Evidence:* **Moderate.** A year-long study of 141 ninth-graders found durable gains, with situational interest as the pathway. Results depend on authenticity.
   - *Example:* "Projectile motion, your way: a batter hits the ball at 30 m/s at 40° — does it clear a 70 m boundary?"
   - *Sources:* [SMU Research](https://blog.smu.edu/research/?p=6799); [Bernacki & Walkington 2018](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/edpsych_fac_articles/190)

9. **Post-reveal "whoa" layer (relevant, optional, one tap).**
   - *What:* After an answer is revealed, one optional line links the concept to something surprising in the real world. It stays strictly on-concept, never interrupts the core lesson, and the student controls the pace.
   - *Evidence:* **Moderate** for post-answer interest helping adolescent memory (one study, n = 60), with a **Strong** constraint against irrelevant content (seductive details, g = −0.33).
   - *Example:* After Total Internal Reflection: "Whoa: this exact effect is how optical fibres carry your internet under the ocean. (tap for 1 diagram)"
   - *Sources:* [Fandakova & Gruber 2021](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/132276/); [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200319103223.htm)

10. **Mistake Case Files (the Mistake Notebook as a detective story).**
    - *What:* Recurring errors are grouped into "cases". The student predicts the root cause before the AI reveals the pattern, then closes the case by answering 3 fresh items.
    - *Evidence:* **Emerging.** It combines prediction and hypercorrection (well supported) with narrative "game fiction", which mainly affects behaviour rather than learning.
    - *Example:* "Case #7: The Vanishing Minus Sign — 4 of your last 6 Kinematics errors share one culprit. Your guess: units, sign convention, or formula choice?"
    - *Sources:* [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [Brod et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1)

11. **Discovery cards (variable *content*, never variable *value*).**
    - *What:* After a completed review set, the student flips one card from a pool of on-syllabus surprises: the history of a formula, a real-world application, or a common exam trap. No rarity tiers, no currency, no purchases, and nothing is lost by skipping.
    - *Evidence:* **Emerging/Contested.** Uncertainty raises engagement in small studies, but randomness tied to value or money is gambling-adjacent.
    - *Example:* "Review done ✓ — flip today's card: 'Exam trap: molarity uses litres of *solution*, molality uses kg of *solvent*. Here's the 5-second way to tell them apart.'"
    - *Sources:* [Howard-Jones et al. (PDF)](https://www.mrcbndu.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_files/Toward%20a%20Science%20of%20Learning%20Games.pdf); [Shen et al. 2015 †](https://doi.org/10.1086/679418); [Zendle et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599795); [EU DSA guidelines on virtual rewards](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors)

12. **Competence mirror (informational progress instead of points and leaderboards).**
    - *What:* Weekly, specific mastery feedback ("you can now explain…", "your error rate on… halved"), with graphs comparing the student to their own past, never to others. Rewards are unexpected and informational, never pre-announced prizes.
    - *Evidence:* **Moderate–Strong.** Positive feedback raises free-choice motivation (d = +0.33), while contingent tangible rewards undermine it. Leaderboards and points raise output, not intrinsic motivation, and can backfire.
    - *Example:* "This week you went from 3 to 7 Thermodynamics concepts you can explain in your own words. The one still fighting back: entropy — want a mystery on it?"
    - *Sources:* [Deci et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627); [Mekler et al. 2017 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048); [Hanus & Fox 2015 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019); [Sailer et al. 2017 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033)

13. **Forgiving rhythm and stop cues (instead of loss-framed streaks).**
    - *What:*
      - Weekly study-day targets with built-in rest days.
      - No "you lost your streak" messages; streaks are opt-in at most.
      - Focus-timer break nudges with pause-and-save.
      - No endless feeds: every session ends on a clear end screen.
    - *Evidence:* **Moderate** on harms (streak-keeping correlates with problematic phone use, though weakly), plus regulatory consensus (the ICO encourages break nudges; the EU recommends streaks off by default for minors).
    - *Example:* "4 of 5 planned days this week ✓ — Sunday is your rest day, nothing lost. Session complete: 25 min, 18 cards. See you tomorrow at 7 pm?"
    - *Sources:* [van Essen & Van Ouytsel 2023](https://doaj.org/article/7e7e45c958c74b29ba203607b6f4a840); [ICO Standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/ico-codes-of-practice/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques); [European Commission DSA guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors)

14. **Honest curiosity nudges.**
    - *What:*
      - At most one notification a day, at a time the student picks.
      - Each carries a *real question from the student's own notes*, answered on the first screen.
      - Quiet hours by default for minors.
      - No inactivity-triggered "we miss you" messages and no rank-fear messages.
    - *Evidence:* **Moderate.** Experiments show clickbait cuts credibility, including with Indian participants and most for unfamiliar brands. EU guidance warns against artificially timed notifications.
    - *Example:* "From your Biology notes: why do you shiver when you're cold? (answer inside, 15 sec)". Never: "You won't believe what you forgot!!" or "Your rank is slipping!"
    - *Sources:* [IIIT Hyderabad](https://iiit.ac.in/?p=18460); [native-ads credibility experiment](https://aminer.org/pub/64a3f363d68f896efac0c197/native-advertising-and-the-credibility-of-online-publishers); [Hunton on the DSA guidelines](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors)

15. **Anonymous question wall (peer curiosity without likes).**
    - *What:* Shows what other students preparing for the same exam asked about this topic. The student predicts an answer before viewing the mentor-verified explanation. No like counts, no follower numbers, no public ranks.
    - *Evidence:* **Emerging.** It uses Loewenstein's "someone else knows" trigger, and teammates and collaboration support relatedness. Teens' reward circuitry is highly sensitive to likes, so leave likes out.
    - *Example:* "[N] NEET aspirants asked about this chapter. Most-asked: 'Why doesn't heart muscle get tired like leg muscle?' Predict first, then see the answer."
    - *Sources:* [Loewenstein 1994 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75); [Sailer et al. 2017 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033); [Sherman et al. 2016 †](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616645673)

### Inferences
- **Most mechanics plug into existing features:**
  - Confidence check → #2
  - Study kits and quizzes → #1, #3
  - Mistake Notebook → #10
  - AI mentor → #5, #6
  - Career Explorer → #7, #8
  - Habits and focus timer → #13
  - Test Debrief → #2
  - Family Update → informational only, in the language of #12
  - Stress reset → linked from the break nudges in #13
- **The hardest combination to copy** is the confidence-driven "plot twist" engine plus the frontier map, because both run on data Kelvra already collects.
- **Measure learning, not minutes.**
  - Test each mechanic on delayed retention, e.g., a 7-day re-quiz comparing items seen with and without the mechanic.
  - Track voluntary returns *without* a notification, one-tap curiosity ratings ("Want to know?" 1–5) and opt-out rates.
  - Watch for compulsive signals, such as late-night sessions by minors and rapid repeated opens.
- **Expect novelty decay.** Evaluate at 6–10+ weeks and rotate mystery formats.
- **Split by age.** 14–17-year-olds count as "children" under the DPDP Act and the ICO and DSA framings.
  - Ship the conservative defaults to everyone: no loss-framed streaks, quiet hours, no social counts.
  - Offer opt-in extras only to users aged 18+.
- **AI accuracy is an ethics issue too.** LLM-generated "whoa" facts and personalised examples must be verified or linked to a source. A wrong surprising fact is both a seductive detail and a breach of trust.

### Gaps
- None of these mechanics has been tested in an Indian exam-prep app. Their combined effects (e.g., bet-first plus plot-twist plus personalisation) are unknown, and so is how they interact with high exam stress.
- Teen-specific evidence is thin. Beyond Fandakova & Gruber (curiosity, interest and memory at ages 10–14), the OECD age decline, the Snapchat-streak survey and the loot-box surveys, most mechanics extrapolate from adult or college studies.
- Items marked † should be spot-checked before external publication. Direct verification of the blocked hosts (ico.org.uk, depts.washington.edu) requires allowing them in the session's network settings.
