## Q1. What do Indian parents worry about and want from edtech (progress visibility, exam readiness, screen time, wellbeing, value for money)?

### Takeaway
I found no rigorous Indian survey that asks parents what they want to *see* in an edtech parent dashboard. That is a real gap. The available signals point three ways. Parents are anxious about *outcomes and value for money*, because of a backlash over edtech mis-selling and promised mentoring that was never delivered. During COVID, parents wanted *more* structured study time, not less screen time. And in rural India, teens' smartphone use goes more to social media than to study, which feeds parents' worry about distraction.

### Cited Findings
- **Value for money / trust (mis-selling):** Parents told the BBC that services they were promised by Byju's, "one-on-one tutoring and an assigned mentor to assess the child's progress", never materialised. **[Weak: journalism]** — [The News Minute summarising BBC](https://www.thenewsminute.com/atom/bbc-report-questions-byju-s-meteoric-rise-india-158499); parents reported debt from loan-financed multi-year subscriptions — [Scroll.in](https://scroll.in/article/1039797/worst-decision-of-my-life-byjus-accused-of-driving-parents-into-debt-with-predatory-practices); [Context/Thomson Reuters Foundation](https://www.context.news/money-power-people/loss-after-loss-indian-parents-say-byjus-pushed-them-into-debt)
- A LocalCircles survey reported that **69%** of people who had taken online coaching or learning classes had faced issues (as reported; the sample and date were not available in the snippet). **[Weak]** — [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/feature/byjus-to-lido-indias-edtech-sector-is-leaking-complaints-unchecked-power-no-trust/1384795)
- The Government of India raised concerns with edtech firms over alleged malpractices, including aggressive mis-selling to parents. **[Weak: news]** — [Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/edtech-startups-in-india-govt-expresses-concern-over-alleged-malpractices-by-byju-s-other-edtech-companies-edtech-startup-funding-news-209376)
- **Screen time vs. study time (COVID-era, LocalCircles):** In a survey with about 21,300 responses (2020), **49%** of parents of class 9–12 children wanted online classes to run longer than the government's proposed 3-hour cap, and **69%** of class 6–8 parents wanted more than the proposed 1.5-hour cap. **[Moderate: large online panel, self-selected]** — [LocalCircles press release](https://m-www.localcircles.com/a/press/page/online-classes-screen-time-survey); [The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/news/many-indian-parents-want-longer-online-classes-kids-shows-survey-129569)
- A vendor survey (Countingwell) reported that **81%** of parents said their children used dedicated learning apps "all by themselves". **[Weak: vendor survey]** — [IANS Life](https://ianslife.in/entertainment/most-children-use-edtech-apps-their-own-report)
- **Distraction concern is grounded in data (ASER 2024, rural, ages 14–16, the first time ASER measured digital literacy):** About 90% had a smartphone in the household and **82.2%** could use one (boys 85.5%, girls 79.4%). Only about **57%** had used it for educational purposes, against about **76%** for social media. Own-phone ownership was **27%** at age 14 and **37.8%** at age 16. Kerala had over 80% educational use. **[Strong: large household survey, rural only; recall window reportedly "past week"]** — [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/education/in-rural-india-82-2-teens-aged-14-16-know-how-to-use-smartphone-but-only-57-use-it-for-studies/2467560); [Deccan Chronicle](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/news/smartphone-access-education-recovery-in-aser-2024-1857277)
- **Parental pressure is perceived as near-universal in the competitive-exam population:** In a Karnataka sample of 570 pre-university students preparing for entrance exams, **87.0%** reported high perceived parental pressure and **86.0%** reported high academic stress (see Q2). **[Moderate: single cross-sectional study]** — [Manipal Academy research portal](https://researcher.manipal.edu/en/publications/academic-stress-perceived-parental-pressure-and-anxiety-related-t/)

### Inferences
- An Indian parent's core question is probably "Is my money/child's time producing exam readiness?" (an outcome and ROI question). A parent view that answers "is my child on track and putting in consistent effort?" honestly, with no upsell, could turn the post-Byju's distrust into a differentiator.
- Parents seem to accept screen time for *structured study* (LocalCircles), and they fear social-media drift (ASER). So the sharing parents value most may be "time in focused study sessions" rather than app-usage policing, and the student can share it without exposing their whole phone.
- Since the student population already reports very high parental pressure, any raw score feed to parents risks adding fuel. This is the core design tension.

### Gaps
- I found no recent (2023–2026) Indian survey asking parents what they want from edtech progress reports, parent apps or PTM-style updates. I also found no industry report (e.g., RedSeer, KPMG India) with parent-feature data in this search. Primary user research is recommended.
- I found no data on Indian parents' monitoring behaviours (e.g., the share who check a teen's phone), so the comparable Pew figures (Q4) are US-only.
- I found no data on whether Indian parents of 18–24 college students expect academic visibility. This is culturally likely (financial dependence, living at home), but it is unverified.

## Q2. Parental academic pressure, student stress and suicide in India, and protective parental behaviours

### Takeaway
Student suicides in India are high and rising: about 13,000 per year (13,044 in 2022, 13,892 in 2023). In 2023 the Supreme Court explicitly named parental pressure as a driver of coaching-hub suicides, and in July 2025 it issued binding mental-health guidelines for schools, colleges and coaching centres. Indian studies consistently link perceived parental pressure to academic stress, depression and suicidality. Nearly all of these studies are cross-sectional.

### Cited Findings
- **NCRB 2022:** **13,044** student suicides (13,089 in 2021), **7.6%** of 1,70,924 total suicides. **2,248** suicides were attributed to "failure in examination" across all ages. Under-reporting is likely because of stigma and the historical criminalisation of attempted suicide (IC3 report analysis). **[Strong: official data, with known under-count]** — [Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/ncrb-report-2023-over-13000-student-suicide-fail-in-exam-girls-national-crime-records-bureau); [Business Today on IC3 report](https://businesstoday.in/education/story/student-suicides-in-india-surge-with-maharashtra-recording-highest-numbers-report-443465-2024-08-29)
- **NCRB 2023** (report published 29 Sep 2025): **13,892** student suicides, the highest in 10 years. **[Strong]** — [Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/ncrb-report-2023-over-13800-student-suicide-deaths-highest-in-10-years-national-crime-records-bureau-maharashtra-up-tn)
- IC3 analysis: student suicides have grown faster than both population growth and total suicides. **[Moderate: NGO analysis of NCRB data]** — [Tribune India](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/india/indias-student-suicide-rate-has-surpassed-overall-trend-population-growth-rate-report)
- **Kota:** At least **26** coaching students died by suicide in Kota in 2023, the highest in at least 8 years (counts vary by source: 24 to 28). **[Weak/Moderate: news]** — [Scroll.in](https://scroll.in/latest/1059745/20-year-old-neet-aspirant-from-west-bengal-dies-by-suicide-in-kota-28th-case-this-year); [The Quint](https://thequint.com/news/education/supreme-court-student-suicides-parents-expectation-coaching-institutes)
- **Supreme Court, 20 Nov 2023:** The bench said parental pressure and high expectations were pushing aspirants to suicide ("The problem is of parents"), and declined to regulate coaching centres in the Malpani PIL. The petitioner argued that blaming parents compounds the problem. **[Moderate: court remarks as reported]** — [Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/education/supreme-court-blames-parent-pressure-on-rising-student-suicides-in-kota); [Careers360 on petitioner](https://news.careers360.com/kota-suicide-supreme-court-sc-pil-neet-jee-coaching-centre-fees-teacher-preparation-doctor-petition)
- **Supreme Court "Saha Guidelines", 25 Jul 2025** (*Sukdeb Saha v. State of Andhra Pradesh*, 2025 LiveLaw (SC) 740): 15 binding interim guidelines under Articles 32/141 covering schools, colleges, hostels and coaching centres. They mandate mental-health policies and support systems and give heightened attention to coaching hubs (Kota, Hyderabad, Chennai, Delhi NCR). The case arose from the death of a 17-year-old NEET aspirant. **[Strong: judgment; full text not reviewed here]** — [LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/supreme-court/in-neet-aspirants-death-case-supreme-court-issues-guidelines-to-protect-mental-health-of-students-in-colleges-coaching-centres-298795); [Oxford Human Rights Hub](https://ohrh.law.ox.ac.uk/the-saha-guidelines-indias-mental-health-leap-as-a-constitutional-right/); [CMHLP](https://cmhlp.org/blogs/a-landmark-ruling-for-student-mental-health-in-india/)
- **Karnataka PU students (n=570, competitive-exam aspirants):** 86.0% had high academic stress, 87.0% high perceived parental pressure, 28.5% moderately high anxiety and 57.7% low general well-being. **[Moderate: cross-sectional]** — [Manipal research portal](https://researcher.manipal.edu/en/publications/academic-stress-perceived-parental-pressure-and-anxiety-related-t/)
- **Pathway model (Indian adolescents):** Parental pressure predicted academic stress, academic stress predicted depression, and depression predicted suicidal ideation (path analysis; Sarma, ASU dissertation, 2014). **[Moderate/Weak: dissertation, cross-sectional path model]** — [ASU repository](https://keep.lib.asu.edu/items/152980)
- **South India test anxiety (n=670, ages 14–18):** Pressure to achieve and trait anxiety were significant risk factors for test anxiety. **[Weak: conference paper]** — [IAFOR](https://acp.iafor.org/?p=13845)

### Inferences
- A parent view that pushes scores, ranks or "behind schedule" alerts to parents of JEE/NEET aspirants could plausibly feed the exact pressure, then stress, then depression pathway described above. The design default should be effort, consistency and wellbeing framing, with *no* rank or percentile push notifications to parents.
- The Saha Guidelines put institutional mental-health duties in the spotlight, so a study app that markets itself to coaching centres/parents should expect scrutiny of features that intensify pressure. Building in crisis resources (e.g., Tele-MANAS 14416) and "how to talk about a bad test" prompts is both protective and defensible.
- Protective parental behaviours (inferred from Q3 evidence and the pressure literature): showing interest in learning rather than marks, talking about plans and the future (academic socialisation), and reacting to failure in a non-catastrophising way. Distal, conversation-based involvement also beats controlling or punitive involvement.

### Gaps
- I found no Indian longitudinal or intervention study showing that *changing* parental behaviour reduces student suicide risk. The evidence is correlational.
- NCRB does not break down student suicides by exam type (JEE/NEET/board) or age band within "students" in the summaries I found.
- I did not access the full text of the 15 Saha Guidelines, so whether any of them speaks directly to parental engagement or edtech is unverified.

## Q3. Self-determination theory: autonomy support vs psychological control, involvement types, and process praise

### Takeaway
The global evidence is strong and consistent. Parental **autonomy support** relates to better achievement and wellbeing. **Psychological control** (guilt, love withdrawal, pressure) relates to anxiety and depression. Among involvement types, **academic socialisation** (talking about the value of education, plans and learning strategies) has the strongest link to achievement in adolescence, while **direct homework help** shows null or negative associations. **Process/effort praise** supports persistence after failure better than ability praise, but replication is mixed. Chinese–US data suggest these patterns hold in collectivist cultures (with smaller autonomy-support effects), which is the closest evidence I found for applying them to India.

### Cited Findings
- **Vasquez, Patall, Fong, Corrigan & Pine (2016)**, "Parent autonomy support, academic achievement, and psychosocial functioning: a meta-analysis of research", *Educational Psychology Review* 28(3), 605–644, doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9329-z. Synthesises **36 studies**. Parent autonomy support was positively related to academic achievement (about 20 studies on achievement-related outcomes) and to adaptive psychosocial functioning, including autonomous motivation. Effects were moderated by grade level and other factors. **[Strong: meta-analysis; exact effect sizes not retrieved]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-015-9329-z); [SDT site PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2016_VasquezPatallFongCorriganPine_EPR.pdf)
- **Hill & Tyson (2009)**, "Parental involvement in middle school: a meta-analytic assessment of the strategies that promote achievement", *Developmental Psychology* 45(3), 740–763. **50 studies**, over 50,000 students. All involvement types except homework help were positively associated with achievement, and **academic socialisation** had the strongest positive association. Homework help was negatively associated (one secondary source reports r ≈ −0.11). The underlying studies are correlational. **[Strong: meta-analysis, correlational]** — [Harvard GSE summary](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/09/10/parental-involvement-puzzle); [PDF via HarvardX](https://courses.edx.org/assets/courseware/v1/afb13fe949b60e70e83a2d18c32dacc0/asset-v1:HarvardX+GSE4x+1T2020+type@asset+block/ParentalInvolvementinMiddleSchoolAMeta-Analysis.pdf); [Texas LD Center summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=301)
- **Wang, Pomerantz & Chen (2007)**, *Child Development* 78(5), 1592–1610 (806 US and Chinese 7th graders, longitudinal). Psychological control predicted poorer emotional functioning in both countries. Autonomy support predicted better emotional and academic functioning in both, but more strongly in the US. Behavioural control (rules and structure) was linked to better academic functioning. Summarised as "universality without uniformity". **[Strong: longitudinal, cross-cultural]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17883450); [Illinois News Bureau](https://news.illinois.edu/study-shows-parenting-styles-have-similar-effects-in-china-and-u-s/)
- **Pinquart (2017)**, meta-analysis of **1,015 studies** on parenting and internalizing symptoms (*Marriage & Family Review* 53, 613–640). Psychological control is associated with internalizing symptoms, and the links are bidirectional (child distress also elicits more control). The companion meta-analysis on externalizing problems found the strongest associations for harsh and psychological control. **[Strong; specific effect sizes not retrieved]** — [PubMed (externalizing, Dev Psych)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28459276/); [Taylor & Francis abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01494929.2016.1247761)
- A meta-analysis of adolescents aged 10–20 (Chyung et al., 2022) found **moderate** associations between perceived parental psychological control and depression/anxiety. An APS 2023 abstract on non-Western samples similarly reported moderate links. **[Moderate]** — [Taylor & Francis](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/01494929.2022.2125481); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/conventions/archive/2023annual/paper/46963/)
- **Stattin & Kerr (2000)**, "Parental monitoring: a reinterpretation", *Child Development* (703 Swedish 14-year-olds). What parents know comes **mainly from the child's voluntary disclosure**, not from parental surveillance or solicitation, and disclosure was the source most tied to lower delinquency. A US replication found the same, and longitudinal work (Kerr, Stattin & Burk 2010) shows that disclosure predicts later parental knowledge. Fletcher et al. (2004) caution that disclosure itself depends on parenting warmth. **[Strong: foundational, replicated]** — [Zendy/DOI 10.1111/1467-8624.00210](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-8624.00210); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2682426)
- **Mueller & Dweck (1998)**, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 75(1), six studies with 5th graders. Children praised for intelligence (vs. effort) showed less persistence and enjoyment, more low-ability attributions and worse performance after failure, and more often misreported their scores. **[Moderate: classic experiment; later replications mixed, e.g., no effect in a vocational-student sample]** — [Columbia PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf); [replication caveat, Educational Psychology](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/01443410.2019.1625306)

### Inferences
- **The disclosure finding (Stattin & Kerr) is the strongest scientific argument for a student-controlled parent view.** Parents get better and more protective knowledge when the teen *chooses* to share, and surveillance does not produce it. A study app should be built as a *disclosure tool*, not a monitoring tool.
- Hill & Tyson suggests the parent view should steer parents toward academic socialisation ("talk about goals, strategies, the plan for the next mock") and *away* from "check whether they did Chapter 7". The parent view should not invite parents to micromanage tasks.
- Wang et al. suggest autonomy-support benefits hold in a collectivist context but may be smaller. So in India, *structure* (agreed routines, study plans the student co-creates) may be an acceptable bridge: Indian parents get structure, and teens keep autonomy over how they meet it.
- Process praise evidence supports showing parents effort and strategy metrics (consistency, sessions completed, topics revised, mistakes reviewed) rather than raw scores. It also supports suggesting effort-focused praise phrasing. The replication caveats mean this should be framed as "low-risk good practice", not a guaranteed effect.

### Gaps
- I found no Indian meta-analysis or large longitudinal study on autonomy support vs psychological control and academic outcomes. The closest cross-cultural evidence is Chinese–US.
- Exact effect sizes for Vasquez 2016 and for Hill & Tyson's academic socialisation estimate were not retrieved. From recall, academic socialisation is roughly r ≈ .39, but that is **unverified** and should be checked in the PDF above before being quoted.
- Gunderson et al. (toddler process praise predicting later mindset) was not located in this search.
- I found no direct evidence on whether *app-delivered* conversation prompts change parent behaviour.

## Q4. Teens' views on parental monitoring apps; covert vs transparent monitoring; what makes sharing acceptable

### Takeaway
Teens strongly dislike parent-centric monitoring apps. They see them as invasive and relationship-damaging, and 76% of teen app-store reviews gave 1 star. Most parental-control features (89%) serve parental control rather than teen self-regulation, and more parental control is associated with *more* online risk, not less. Sharing feels acceptable when it is transparent, reciprocal and teen-initiated, and when it supports the teen's own self-regulation.

### Cited Findings
- **Ghosh, Badillo-Urquiola, Guha, LaViola & Wisniewski (2018)**, "Safety vs. Surveillance: What Children Have to Say about Mobile Apps for Parental Control", CHI '18, doi:10.1145/3173574.3173698. A qualitative analysis of **736 child-written reviews of 37 parental-control apps** on Google Play. Teens found the apps overly restrictive and privacy-invasive and felt they harmed their relationship with parents. Child ratings were far lower than parent ratings, and **76% of child reviews gave 1 star**. The authors call for designs that move beyond monitoring. **[Strong for HCI: peer-reviewed; review data is self-selected]** — [Marquette ePublications](https://epublications.marquette.edu/mscs_fac/618); [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/7883)
- **Ghosh et al. (2018)**, "A Matter of Control or Safety?" (CHI '18 extended abstract): In **215 parent–teen dyads**, greater parental use of monitoring apps was associated with *more* online risks for teens, not fewer, and with lower teen–parent relationship quality (per the abstract). **[Moderate: survey, correlational]** — [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/7882); [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-matter-of-control-or-safety-examining-parental-use-of-technical/)
- **Wisniewski, Ghosh, Xu, Rosson & Carroll (2017)**, "Parental Control vs. Teen Self-Regulation: Is There a Middle Ground for Mobile Online Safety?", CSCW '17. A feature analysis of **75 Android apps**. **89%** of features supported parental control and **11%** supported teen self-regulation; by one report, risk-coping was 4%, self-monitoring 2% and impulse control <1%. The paper proposes the TOSS framework, which pairs parental strategies (monitoring, restriction, active mediation) with teen strategies (self-monitoring, impulse control, risk coping). **[Strong for HCI]** — [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/7430); [Penn State News](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/online-security-apps-focus-parental-control-not-teen-self-regulation); [paper PDF](https://htirlab.github.io/papers/parental_control.pdf)
- A Value Sensitive Design prototype (Ghosh dissertation work) was rated by both parents and teens as less privacy-invasive and better for trust than parent-centric apps. **[Moderate: prototype study]** — [UCF STARS ETD](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/etd/6053); [VSD paper](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/4491)
- **Pew Research Center (US):** In 2016, among parents of 13–17-year-olds, **48%** had looked through their teen's call records/messages, **39%** used parental controls to block, filter or monitor, **16%** used location tracking on the teen's phone, and **43%** knew their teen's phone password. Monitoring was more intensive for 13–14-year-olds than for 15–17-year-olds. **[Strong: national survey, but dated and US]** — [Pew 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2016/01/07/parents-teens-and-digital-monitoring/)
- Pew 2024 (headline only, page not fetchable here): about **half of parents** say they look through their teen's phone, and **43% of teens** think their parent checks their phone. **[Moderate: headline verified via search snippet only]** — [Pew, 29 Feb 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/02/29/how-teens-and-parents-approach-screen-time/)

### Inferences
- The covert vs transparent distinction matters. Teens' objections centre on secrecy, invasiveness and loss of control. A view where the teen sees *exactly* what the parent sees (a "parent's eye preview") directly addresses this.
- "Monitoring correlates with more risk" (Ghosh) is consistent with Stattin & Kerr: surveillance may displace disclosure. For a study app, adding surveillance features (screenshots, app-usage logs, location) would likely trigger app abandonment and workarounds, such as a second phone or fake sessions.
- Acceptable sharing conditions synthesised from the HCI work: teen-initiated, visible to the teen, reciprocal (parents also commit to something), coarse-grained, supportive of self-regulation, and revocable.

### Gaps
- I found no Indian HCI study on teen attitudes to parental monitoring apps. Indian family norms (greater acceptance of parental authority, shared devices) may raise tolerance, but that is **unverified**.
- I could not access the Common Sense Media data on teen attitudes to monitoring in this session.
- I found no studies specific to *academic* monitoring apps (as opposed to online-safety apps). Inferring from safety apps is a reasonable but imperfect proxy.

## Q5. Existing family-sharing design patterns (Google Family Link, Apple Screen Time, digests) and their criticisms, plus the Indian legal context

### Takeaway
Mainstream family tools have swung between child autonomy and parental authority. Google let supervised children end supervision at 13, then reversed this in January 2026 after parent backlash, and now requires parental approval. The episode shows the tension Kelvra must handle by age band. India's DPDP Act treats everyone under 18 as a child. It requires verifiable parental consent and restricts "tracking or behavioural monitoring" of children, which strongly favours a minimal, purpose-bound and transparent parent view.

### Cited Findings
- **Google Family Link (Jan 2026):** Google had been emailing children near 13 to say they could soon remove parental supervision. Parents and a child-rights group objected, saying continued supervision should be the default. Google then changed policy so that supervised minors need **parental approval** to turn off supervision, effective globally. Earlier, a child removing supervision without permission caused the device to be "temporarily" locked. **[Moderate: tech press, multiple outlets]** — [Bitdefender](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/google-backtracks-on-teen-parental-controls-after-public-backlash); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/1/14/child-rights-org-says-google-undermines-parental-control-of-child-accounts); [PiunikaWeb](https://piunikaweb.com/2026/01/12/google-emails-kids-drop-parental-controls/)
- User complaints include 18-year-olds unable to turn off Family Link supervision because of system errors. This illustrates the cost of not handling the transition to adulthood cleanly. **[Weak: complaint sites / tech news]** — [Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-family-link-bug-3663231/); [Sikayetvar](https://www.sikayetvar.com/en/family-link-us/im-18-but-cant-turn-off-google-family-link-supervision-due-to-a-system-error)
- **India DPDP Act 2023, Section 9:** Processing data of a "child" (under **18**) requires **verifiable parental consent**. Section 9(3) bars **tracking or behavioural monitoring** of children and targeted advertising directed at them, subject to exemptions. The DPDP Rules were notified **14 Nov 2025** with about 18 months of phased enforcement (around May 2027). Rule 10 covers verification. The Fourth Schedule exempts educational institutions for teaching and safety purposes, but commentators **disagree** on whether commercial edtech vendors qualify. **[Moderate: legal commentary; verify against the gazette]** — [LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/articles/dpdp-child-data-rules-demand-edtech-platforms-555317); [S.S. Rana & Co.](https://ssrana.in/articles/schools-coaching-centres-platforms-processing-childrens-data-are-you-compliant/); [Bar & Bench on draft rules](https://www.barandbench.com/view-point/childs-personal-data-and-privacy-analysing-the-draft-dpdp-rules-2025); [DPDP Rule 10 text](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html)

### Inferences
- For **under-18 users in India**, a parent link is likely *legally necessary* (verifiable parental consent), but sending detailed behavioural data to parents may itself sit uneasily with the S.9(3) spirit. Aggregated, purpose-limited summaries are the safer pattern. Legal review is required.
- For **18+ users**, parents have no legal standing, so all sharing must be opt-in, student-controlled and revocable at any time without notifying the parent in a punitive way. On a student's 18th birthday, the app should prompt them to re-choose their sharing settings (learning from the Family Link complaints).
- The Family Link reversal shows that Indian and global parents may push back on "the teen can switch it off" for minors. A middle path for 14–17-year-olds: the parent link exists with consent, but the *content* shared is negotiated, and the teen always sees what is shared.
- Weekly digest emails/WhatsApp summaries (a common pattern) suit Indian parents, who rely heavily on WhatsApp, but they must contain effort and wellbeing framing, not marks-first tables.

### Gaps
- Apple Screen Time/Family Sharing criticisms and teen-transparency features were not researched in depth here because of the tool-call budget.
- I found no evaluation data (e.g., trust or retention outcomes) on weekly digest formats in edtech.
- I found no Indian court or regulator guidance specifically on parent dashboards in edtech.

## Q6. Design principles for a consent-based, privacy-respecting parent view (synthesis)

### Takeaway
The evidence converges on one stance: **treat the parent view as a structured disclosure channel the student controls, aimed at prompting supportive conversations, not a surveillance dashboard.** The principles below are each tied to the evidence above.

### Cited Findings (principle → evidence)
1. **Student chooses what to share; default is minimal; revocable.** Parental knowledge comes mainly from voluntary disclosure — [Stattin & Kerr 2000](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-8624.00210). Monitoring correlates with more risk and lower trust — [Ghosh et al. 2018](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/7882). For 18+, sharing is purely opt-in. For under-18s in India, verifiable parental consent is required, but the content shared can be teen-negotiated — [LiveLaw on DPDP](https://www.livelaw.in/articles/dpdp-child-data-rules-demand-edtech-platforms-555317).
2. **Full transparency: the student sees exactly what the parent sees ("preview as parent"), and nothing is covert.** Teens reject invasive, secretive apps (76% 1-star) — [Ghosh et al. CHI 2018](https://epublications.marquette.edu/mscs_fac/618).
3. **Summaries, not raw data.** Weekly consistency, focus-session counts and a topics-covered trend, with no per-minute logs, screenshots, app usage or location. This keeps the view purpose-limited, in line with the DPDP "no behavioural monitoring of children" principle — [S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/articles/schools-coaching-centres-platforms-processing-childrens-data-are-you-compliant/); it also aligns with self-regulation-first design — [Wisniewski et al. CSCW 2017](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/7430).
4. **Effort/process-first, score-optional.** Show effort, strategies and improvement. Mock-test scores are shared only if the student opts in, and with context (trend, not rank). Ability praise undermines persistence after failure — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf); parental pressure is linked to stress and suicidality in India — [Manipal study](https://researcher.manipal.edu/en/publications/academic-stress-perceived-parental-pressure-and-anxiety-related-t/), [Sarma 2014](https://keep.lib.asu.edu/items/152980).
5. **Conversation prompts instead of commands.** Each digest includes 1–2 suggested questions for parents (e.g., "What topic felt hardest this week, and what's your plan for it?") and effort-praise phrasing. Academic socialisation has the strongest link to achievement, and homework help is negative — [Hill & Tyson 2009](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/09/10/parental-involvement-puzzle).
6. **Autonomy-supportive structure.** Let the student co-create a study plan and share *the plan and progress against it*, which gives parents the structure they want without control over the method. Autonomy support relates to achievement and wellbeing — [Vasquez et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-015-9329-z). Behavioural structure is OK, but psychological control harms, including in China — [Wang et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17883450).
7. **Two-way and reciprocal.** The student can add a note ("Tough week, need a break Sunday"), and parents can send encouragement only, with no task assignment or nagging buttons. This builds on the TOSS "active mediation" pairing — [Wisniewski et al. 2017](https://htirlab.github.io/papers/parental_control.pdf).
8. **No alarmist push notifications to parents.** No "your child scored low" or "missed a session" alerts. Use a calm weekly cadence, which reduces pressure triggers in a population with about 13,000 student suicides per year — [NCRB via Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/ncrb-report-2023-over-13800-student-suicide-deaths-highest-in-10-years-national-crime-records-bureau-maharashtra-up-tn).
9. **Wellbeing layer and crisis resources.** Include a parent guide on exam stress and how to respond to a bad result, and India helplines. This aligns with the Supreme Court's 2025 Saha Guidelines emphasis on student mental-health support — [LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/supreme-court/in-neet-aspirants-death-case-supreme-court-issues-guidelines-to-protect-mental-health-of-students-in-colleges-coaching-centres-298795).
10. **Clean age transitions.** At 18, the student re-confirms or ends sharing, avoiding Family Link-style lock-in complaints — [Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-family-link-bug-3663231/).
11. **Honest value signal for parents (anti-mis-selling).** No upsell in the parent view, and no fabricated "readiness %". Parents' distrust stems from undelivered mentoring and progress tracking — [The News Minute/BBC](https://www.thenewsminute.com/atom/bbc-report-questions-byju-s-meteoric-rise-india-158499).

### Inferences
- A tiered model fits the age range:
  - **14–17:** The parent link is consent-required. Effort summaries are on by default, scores are opt-in, and teens see everything shared.
  - **18–24:** No link unless the student invites one. Every item is opt-in, and the student can pause sharing any time (e.g., an "exam week, pausing updates" status).
- WhatsApp-style weekly digests in Indian languages are likely to reach parents better than in-app dashboards (unverified; worth testing).
- The core business tension is that parents often pay while students use the app. Evidence suggests trust from the student drives honest data, and honest data is what makes the parent view valuable.

### Gaps
- I found no RCTs testing student-controlled vs parent-controlled academic dashboards for outcomes (grades, wellbeing, retention).
- I found no Indian user research on teen acceptance of specific parent-view features. Recommended next step: dyad interviews with JEE/NEET/board students and parents (Tier 1 and Tier 2/3 cities), plus card-sorting on "what I'd share".
- Whether S.9(3) DPDP "behavioural monitoring" covers study-effort summaries is a legal question that is unresolved here.
