# Learning-science evidence for Kelvra behaviour and response features (answer-writing coach, confidence check, habit coach, focus tracker)

Evidence labels used below: **[STRONG]** = multiple meta-analyses / large RCTs; **[MODERATE]** = one meta-analysis or several consistent studies; **[WEAK]** = single/small studies, lab-only, or secondary-source figure; **[CONTESTED]** = failed replications or conflicting meta-analyses. Figures marked "(secondary)" were found via a secondary summary and should be checked against the original paper before being quoted publicly. Citations marked "(from prior knowledge, not re-verified in this session)" are well-known papers whose details were not re-fetched here.

## 1. Answer-writing coach: feedback, rubrics, model answers, written retrieval, AI grading

### Takeaway
Feedback works on average (d ~0.4-0.5) but varies hugely, and a large minority of feedback interventions make performance worse. Feedback helps most when it is about the task and the process, tells the student the next step, and is acted on in a revision. Automated writing feedback has a pooled effect of g ~0.36-0.55. LLM feedback is roughly as good as human feedback for low-stakes formative use, but LLM *scores* are only fair to moderately reliable. The design should therefore lead with feedback and revision, not with a precise grade. Unguarded AI help can lower independent performance.

### Cited Findings
**General feedback evidence**
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), meta-analysis of 435 studies (N > 61,000): average feedback effect **d = 0.48**, with significant heterogeneity, so "feedback cannot be understood as a single consistent form of treatment." The information content of the feedback matters most. Effects are larger for cognitive and motor outcomes than for motivational or behavioural ones. Praise, punishment and rewards are low-effect; richer information is higher-effect. [STRONG] — [Frontiers in Psychology 10:3087, PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456); [UZH reference page](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Wisniewski-et-al.-(2020)). Critics of the Hattie programme have questioned how studies were included — [critical blog, not neutral](https://visablelearning.blogspot.com/p/feedback-revisited.html).
- Kluger & DeNisi (1996), Psychological Bulletin 119(2): 607 effect sizes, average **d ≈ 0.41**, but **over one-third of feedback interventions decreased performance**. Feedback that draws attention to the self (ego, comparison, praise of the person) rather than the task tends to reduce effects. [STRONG] — [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) (from prior knowledge, not re-verified in this session)
- Hattie & Timperley (2007), Review of Educational Research 77(1): good feedback answers three questions: "Where am I going?" (goal), "How am I going?" (progress) and "Where to next?" (next step). It works at four levels: task, process, self-regulation and self. Self-level praise ("you're smart") is the least effective. Process-level and self-regulation-level feedback produce deeper learning. [Influential review, conceptual] — [doi:10.3102/003465430298487](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487) (from prior knowledge)

**Rubrics and self-assessment**
- Panadero, Jonsson, Pinedo & Fernández-Castilla (2023), Educational Psychology Review: after correcting for publication bias, rubrics have a moderate effect on academic performance, **g = 0.45 (k = 21)**. The effect on self-regulated learning is small and not significant (**g = 0.23**, 95% CI [-0.15, 0.60], 5 studies). The effect on self-efficacy is g = 0.18 (3 studies, ns). Most moderators were non-significant. [MODERATE] — [doi:10.1007/s10648-023-09823-4 / Deusto repository](https://deustoteka.deusto.es/items/59022074-df96-4c6a-8c52-3aa513c0ae3e); [UNED record](https://portalcientifico.uned.es/documentos/657f1b373ea324404509cf90)
- A separate meta-analysis co-authored by Panadero found that monitoring tools (journals, portfolios, rubrics) have a low effect on self-regulated learning: d = 0.19 across 32 studies (secondary, via search summary). [WEAK–MODERATE] — [Panadero rubric meta-analysis record](https://deustoteka.deusto.es/items/59022074-df96-4c6a-8c52-3aa513c0ae3e)

**Written / elaborative retrieval practice (why making students write full answers is worth it)**
- Dunlosky et al. (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1): of 10 techniques, only **practice testing and distributed practice** were rated "high utility." Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation were rated "moderate." Highlighting, rereading and summarisation were rated "low." [STRONG] — [doi:10.1177/1529100612453266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) (from prior knowledge)
- The testing effect meta-analyses report Rowland (2014, Psych Bull) **g ≈ 0.50** and Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, RER) **g ≈ 0.61**. Effects are larger when feedback follows the test. [STRONG] — [Rowland 2014 doi:10.1037/a0037559](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559); [Adesope et al. 2017 doi:10.3102/0034654316689306](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306) (from prior knowledge)

**Automated writing evaluation (AWE) and AI feedback**
- Fleckenstein, Liebenow & Meyer (2023), Frontiers in AI: three-level meta-analysis of 20 studies (k = 84, N = 2,828). Automated feedback has a **medium effect on writing performance, g = 0.55**. Heterogeneity was large: "automated feedback tools cannot be understood as a single consistent form of intervention." No moderator comparison reached significance. [MODERATE] — [PMC10351274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10351274/); [IPN summary](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/automated-feedback-and-writing)
- A three-level meta-analysis of AWE in EFL/ESL writing (24 studies, 1993–2021) found **g = 0.59** between groups. [MODERATE] — [record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c338t-84cd5941e3b613bfb66f917b1bda208382e9a5f9c22049803b237264ae9818333/Details)
- Scherer, Graham & Busse, Assessing Writing vol. 68: 33 studies up to the end of 2024. Algorithm-based feedback gives **g = 0.36** overall. Grammarly and Pigai were significant, but **ChatGPT feedback effects were non-significant**. L2 learners benefit most, and **transfer to new writing tasks was small**. [MODERATE; figures secondary] — [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/can-algorithm-based-feedback-help-students-to-write-better-a-meta/)
- Kaliisa et al. (2025), Educational Psychology: 41 studies, 4,813 students. **No significant difference in learning performance between AI-generated and human feedback**. The writing/language subgroup showed the same, with very high heterogeneity (I² = 95%). [MODERATE; secondary] — [search summary source, ASCD](https://ascd.org/el/articles/how-effective-is-ai-in-giving-writing-feedback)
- Steiss et al. (2024), Learning and Instruction 91:101894: 200 human and 200 ChatGPT feedback pieces on the same secondary-school essays. Trained humans gave higher-quality feedback on every dimension **except criteria-based feedback**, where they were equal. The gaps were modest (about 3.6 vs 4.0 on a 5-point scale). Quality differences depended on essay quality: humans had an edge for the strongest and weakest writers. [WEAK–MODERATE, single study] — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-writing-ai-feedback/); [KQED MindShift](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/63901/writing-researcher-finds-ai-feedback-better-than-i-thought)

**LLM scoring accuracy (risk of wrong grading)**
- Studies from 2024 show wide variation in how well ChatGPT scores agree with human raters:
  - For secondary-school essays, ChatGPT scores were not significantly different from human scores, which the authors judged "may be sufficient for low-stakes, formative assessment."
  - Other studies report moderate-to-low reliability for L2 writing, unacceptable intra-rater (run-to-run) consistency for EFL compositions, and poor agreement for college essays with GPT-3.5.
  - GPT-4o showed only "fair" agreement on 240 argumentative essays, with scores significantly lower and inconsistent.
  - LLMs in general score lower than humans on ASAP essays, with weak correlations to human scores.
  - [CONTESTED / model-dependent] — [Shermis arXiv 2408.09540](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.09540v1); [ChatGPT EFL reliability](https://www.shanlaxjournals.in/journals/index.php/education/article/view/7855); [arXiv 2409.13120](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2409.13120); [holistic essay scoring](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-b9740b2f8a7544f88e4e523afe5e7e0b/Description)

**Over-reliance risk**
- Bastani et al. (2025), PNAS 122(26) e2422633122: field experiment with about 1,000 Turkish high-school maths students. Unrestricted GPT-4 ("GPT Base") improved practice performance, but **grades fell by 17% once access was removed**, compared with students who never had access, because students used it as a crutch. A guardrailed "GPT Tutor" that gave hints, not answers, largely mitigated the harm. Limitations: one school, one subject. [MODERATE, large single RCT] — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2422633122); [Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)

### Inferences
- The main value of the coach is **retrieval practice plus actionable feedback plus revision**, not the score. Because LLM scores drift, a single numeric score tracked over time is the weakest part of the design. Show score *bands*, rubric-criterion levels and a trend, not decimals. Consider running the grader more than once and taking the median, or flagging low agreement between runs.
- Criteria-based feedback is where LLMs most matched humans (Steiss). This supports anchoring every comment to an explicit, board-specific rubric criterion (CBSE/ICSE/state board marking schemes, UPSC mains-style rubrics).
- Follow Kluger & DeNisi and Hattie & Timperley:
  - Never use self-level or comparative feedback ("you're weak at writing", leaderboards on answer quality).
  - Use the format "what was good on criterion X → the one biggest gap → a concrete next step → rewrite this paragraph now."
  - Limit feedback to 1–3 priority points.
- The model answer should come **after** the student's attempt and self-assessment, never before, so students do not copy it. Show it as a side-by-side comparison against the rubric. Panadero's small and non-significant effect of rubrics on self-regulated learning suggests students need prompting to actually use the rubric for self-assessment, for example: "Rate yourself on each criterion before seeing the AI score."
- Design against the Bastani crutch effect: the coach must not write the answer for the student. The AI should give hints or questions, and the student does the rewrite.
- Transfer to new tasks is small (Scherer et al.). Rotate question types and topics rather than polishing one answer forever.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous studies on AI feedback for **spoken/viva answers**, or on whether reducing **filler words** affects oral exam outcomes. Evidence on oral assessment is mostly about assessment validity and anxiety in higher education, not practice tools. Treat speech metrics (filler count, pace) as engagement features without evidence of learning benefit.
- I found no India-specific studies of AWE or LLM feedback for board or competitive exam answer writing (e.g., CBSE long answers, UPSC Mains), nor validation of LLM scoring against Indian board marking schemes. Kelvra would need its own validation study comparing its scores with teacher scores.
- I could not verify the exact numbers from the Kaliisa 2025 and Scherer et al. papers against their full texts.
- There is no meta-analytic evidence isolating "model answer comparison" against rubric-only feedback for exam answer writing. Worked-example research (Sweller) is mostly about procedural problem solving in maths and science.

## 2. Confidence check and metacognitive calibration

### Takeaway
Asking for confidence before revealing the answer is well grounded. High-confidence errors that are corrected with feedback are remembered better (the hypercorrection effect), and judgments help students choose what to restudy. However, the evidence that calibration feedback improves *grades*, as opposed to calibration itself, is mixed. Feedback must be specific and repeated, and low performers stay overconfident the longest.

### Cited Findings
- **Hypercorrection effect:** errors made with high confidence are *more* likely to be corrected after feedback than low-confidence errors (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, 2006). The effect has been replicated many times by independent groups, including Fazio & Marsh, Sitzman & Rhodes, and Butler et al. The proposed mechanism is surprise-driven attention to the corrective feedback. A competing account is that confident errors come from people with more domain knowledge, but a residual effect remains after controlling for difficulty. [STRONG for the phenomenon; no meta-analysis found] — [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006 PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/357328401.pdf); [PMC4084803](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4084803); [PMC3970786](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970786); [PMC3079415](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079415); [Sitzman & Rhodes](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-013-0344-3.pdf)
- **Low-confidence correct answers also need attention.** Griffiths & Higham ("Beyond hypercorrection") examined correction of low-confidence errors and the role of feedback attention. [WEAK–MODERATE] — [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/412186/1/Beyond_Hypercorrection_manuscipt_R2_Final.docx)
- **Calibration feedback in classrooms:**
  - Callender, Franco-Watkins & Roberts (2016, Metacognition and Learning): exam performance improved and overconfidence decreased on Exam 2 **only for students who received feedback about both their performance and their judgments**. [WEAK–MODERATE] — [doi:10.1007/s11409-015-9142-6](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11409-015-9142-6)
  - Another study found that only salient feedback and a "motivation warning" lecture improved prediction accuracy. Review sessions, incentives and reflection did not. [WEAK] — [ERIC EJ1098426](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1098426.pdf)
  - Miller & Geraci (2011): incentives with minimal feedback produced **no** improvement in metacognition or performance. With more concrete feedback, calibration improved for low performers, **but exam scores did not**. [WEAK] — [doi:10.1007/s11409-011-9083-7](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11409-011-9083-7)
  - Low performers keep significantly overestimating their scores despite practice-test feedback. When they do improve calibration, it is by lowering their confidence, not by raising their scores. [WEAK–MODERATE] — [Improving metacognition in the classroom (Univ. Kentucky)](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/improving-metacognition-in-the-classroom-through-instruction-trai/)
- A 2022 meta-analysis, "Calibrating calibration" (Journal of Educational Psychology 114(4), 681–700), synthesises learning-strategy instruction interventions aimed at monitoring accuracy. I could not retrieve its effect sizes. [Unverified] — (title found in search; see Gaps)
- **Dunning–Kruger:** Kruger & Dunning (1999) found that bottom-quartile performers overestimate themselves most. Later work argues that much of the classic pattern is a statistical artefact (regression to the mean, better-than-average bias). Gignac & Zajenkowski (2020, Intelligence) found little support for the effect once these artefacts are handled properly. The robust classroom fact is simpler: **low performers are more overconfident in their exam predictions**. [CONTESTED as a psychological mechanism] — [Kruger & Dunning 1999 doi:10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1121](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1121); [Gignac & Zajenkowski 2020 doi:10.1016/j.intell.2020.101449](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2020.101449) (from prior knowledge)

### Inferences
- The **pre-answer confidence tap (e.g., Guess / Unsure / Sure)** is cheap and justified on two grounds. When a "Sure" answer turns out wrong, show the correction prominently and briefly ask the student to restate it, which exploits hypercorrection. Then schedule that item for an early re-test.
- Calibration feedback must be **specific, repeated and tied to both judgment and performance**: "You were 'Sure' on 20 questions and got 13 right (65%)." Generic reflection prompts and incentives did not work.
- Track a simple calibration curve (confidence bucket vs accuracy) per subject over weeks. Do not promise that this will raise marks: the evidence mainly supports better study allocation and fewer overconfidence errors.
- Avoid labelling a student "overconfident" (self-level feedback, ego threat), especially teenagers. Frame it as a fact about the questions: "Your 'Sure' answers in Organic Chemistry are less reliable than in Physics. Re-check these."
- Low performers need the most help and resist it the most. Pair confidence data with concrete actions, such as auto-adding high-confidence errors to spaced review, rather than only showing a chart.
- Do not use confidence-weighted *scoring* (penalising confident wrong answers) as the main score. It can add anxiety, although Indian competitive exams already have negative marking, so an optional "negative marking mode" practice could fit students' context. This is an inference only; I found no evidence on it.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve effect sizes from the 2022 "Calibrating calibration" meta-analysis.
- I found no meta-analysis directly testing whether calibration feedback improves *grades*. The primary studies are small and mixed.
- I found no India-specific or adolescent-specific calibration-training studies. Most samples are US undergraduates.
- I found no direct evidence on confidence-weighted testing in apps for Indian exam takers.

## 3. Habit and behaviour coach: cramming detection, implementation intentions, WOOP, self-monitoring, streaks

### Takeaway
The components have strong support:
- Spaced practice beats cramming.
- "If-then" plans have medium-to-large effects on goal attainment (d ~0.65).
- Self-monitoring with *recorded* progress helps (d ~0.40).
- MCII (WOOP) has small-to-moderate effects with students.

Habits take about two months to become automatic, with very wide individual variation, so a "one experiment per week" framing should present experiments as practice toward habits, not as finished habits. Streaks are powerful but can backfire when broken.

### Cited Findings
- **Distributed vs massed practice:** Cepeda et al. (2006), Psychological Bulletin, synthesised 839 assessments from 317 experiments. Spaced practice outperforms massed practice robustly, and the optimal gap scales with the retention interval. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate distributed practice "high utility." [STRONG] — [Cepeda et al. 2006 doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354); [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) (from prior knowledge)
- **Implementation intentions:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006) meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found a medium-to-large effect, **d = 0.65**. The **academic-goal subgroup had d = 0.72**, mid-range across domains (d = 0.41–1.12). [STRONG overall; academic figure secondary] — [secondary summary via e-mentor](https://www.e-mentor.edu.pl/eng/article/index/number/72/id/1323); original: Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38, 69–119, [doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)
- A newer meta-analysis by Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024) covered 642 independent tests and built a taxonomy of cues and responses. I did not retrieve its education-specific results. — [summary](https://siliconcanals.com/?p=107751)
- **Classroom caveat:** earlier meta-analyses rely heavily on lab paradigms. Real-classroom tests are mixed. In one dissertation, simply reminding students to do homework outperformed reminder plus implementation intention. [WEAK] — [ASEE paper](https://peer.asee.org/an-introductory-study-of-the-impact-of-implementation-intentions-on-assignment-completion-rates-with-an-emphasis-on-engineering-technology-students.pdf); [MSU dissertation](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/1924/FULL_TEXT/view)
- **MCII / WOOP with adolescents:**
  - Duckworth, Grant, Loew, Oettingen & Gollwitzer (2011), Educational Psychology: 66 high-school students preparing for a high-stakes exam (PSAT) were randomised to a 30-minute written MCII exercise or a placebo. The MCII group completed **more than 60% more practice questions**. The outcome was practice volume, not test score, and the sample was small. [WEAK–MODERATE] — [Duckworth et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/duckworth_self-regulation_11.pdf); [publisher](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01443410.2010.506003)
  - Duckworth, Kirby, Gollwitzer & Oettingen (2013): 77 middle-school students. MCII improved grades, attendance and conduct compared with a positive-thinking control. [WEAK–MODERATE] — [PDF](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/duckworth-kirby-oettingen-2013.pdf)
  - Most MCII studies use limited comparison conditions (Abdulla & Woods 2021). — [RGU repository](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1205840)
- **Self-monitoring:** Harkin et al. (2016), Psychological Bulletin 142(2), meta-analysis of 138 RCTs (N = 19,951). Monitoring interventions greatly increased monitoring (**d = 1.98**) and improved goal attainment (**d ≈ 0.40**). Effects were larger when progress was **physically recorded** (d = 0.43 vs 0.29 unrecorded) and when it was **reported** (private reported d = 0.47 vs 0.19 unreported). Most studies were in health domains. [STRONG; moderator figures secondary] — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/87431); [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals.aspx)
- **Habit formation time:** Lally et al. (2010), European Journal of Social Psychology: 96 participants. Median time to reach 95% of peak automaticity was **66 days, range 18–254**. Missing a single day did not materially derail habit formation. [MODERATE, single study] — [doi:10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) (from prior knowledge, not re-verified in this session)
- **Procrastination interventions:** van Eerde & Klingsieck (2018), Educational Research Review, meta-analysis found that CBT-based interventions had the largest effects in reducing procrastination. Self-regulation and time-management training effects were smaller and more variable. [MODERATE] — [doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.002) (from prior knowledge; exact effect sizes not re-verified)
- **Streaks:**
  - Silverman & Barasch (2023), Journal of Consumer Research: people are strongly motivated by streaks, even artificial ones. Once a streak breaks, persistence drops, and **highlighting the broken streak makes it worse**. Offering a way to repair the streak softens the drop. Users can come to value the streak over the underlying activity (goal displacement). [MODERATE, lab/consumer experiments] — [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive); [Univ. Delaware](https://lerner.udel.edu/seeing-opportunity/lerner-professor-researches-how-streaks-motivate-us/); [Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)
  - Causal evidence in learning is thin. A 2026 Northwestern working paper tests streaks on a maths platform with 4th–6th graders in Peru (results not retrieved). Khan Academy reportedly retired its streak feature over motivation concerns. [WEAK] — [IPR WP-26-05](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2026/wp-26-05.pdf); [Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)

### Inferences
- **Cramming detection** is well justified: when study minutes cluster in the 1–3 days before tests, the app can say so. The intervention should be a *specific* spacing plan (auto-scheduled reviews), not a scolding message.
- **Weekly habit experiment:** use the template "When [specific cue: after dinner / when I sit at my desk at 7 pm], I will [specific small action: do 10 recall questions]."
  - Add a WOOP step first (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan), since MCII outperformed positive visualisation with teens.
  - Keep each experiment small and log whether the "if" happened.
  - Lally's data suggest a week is enough to test feasibility, not to form a habit. Plan for experiments to repeat across 6–10 weeks.
- **Self-monitoring:** have students record progress visibly and review it weekly. Harkin's moderator findings (recorded and reported > unrecorded) support a weekly review screen. Sharing with a parent or mentor could count as "reported," but public reporting rests on only 3 studies, and with Indian parents it could feel coercive. Keep sharing opt-in for students old enough to decide.
- **Streaks:**
  - Use forgiving consistency metrics ("5 of the last 7 days") rather than fragile day-streaks.
  - If a streak exists, include repair or freeze options and never highlight a broken streak.
  - Lally found single misses don't matter for habit formation, so the app's messaging should say so explicitly.
  - Watch for goal displacement: a student opening the app for 30 seconds just to keep a streak.
- "Best study time" detection is a reasonable self-monitoring insight. However, I found no evidence that matching study to chronotype or detected best time improves outcomes in this population, so present it as a suggestion, not a prescription.

### Gaps
- I found no education-specific meta-analysis of implementation intentions, and could not retrieve education subgroup results from Sheeran et al. (2024).
- I found no Indian-sample studies of implementation intentions, WOOP or streaks.
- Results of the Peru streak RCT were not retrieved.
- Exact effect sizes for van Eerde & Klingsieck (2018) were not re-verified in this session.
- I found no evidence on automated cramming *detection* plus nudges specifically, as opposed to spacing in general.

## 4. Focus and distraction tracker: multitasking, phone presence, task switching, Pomodoro, self-monitoring

### Takeaway
Media multitasking is reliably but modestly associated with lower achievement (r ≈ -0.25, correlational). Switching tasks has real performance costs. The "mere phone presence" brain-drain effect is contested: it survives in meta-analyses only weakly, mostly for working memory. Fixed-interval (Pomodoro-like) breaks mainly improve mood and efficiency, not output, and later studies conflict. Self-monitoring and brief reflection are the best-supported parts of this feature.

### Cited Findings
- **Media multitasking and achievement:**
  - A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 correlational studies found academic media multitasking correlated about **r ≈ -0.25** with achievement (secondary report). [MODERATE, correlational] — [CEEOL record](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1336948); [St. Louis Today report](https://www.stltoday.com/exclusive/article_00a14e40-57d5-5176-8da4-50d017951972.html)
  - A 2018 review of 38 studies (2003–2017) linked media multitasking to lower GPA, test performance, recall and reading comprehension. [MODERATE] — [Springer/related review](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09746-0.pdf)
  - Cvetković et al. meta-analysis: heterogeneity I² ≈ 94%. — [Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-01028-x)
- **Heavy multitaskers and distractibility:** Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein (2017) found only a weak link (**d = 0.17**) between heavier media multitasking and distractibility, possibly negligible after correcting for small-study effects. [CONTESTED] — [Wiradhany 2017](https://www.scribd.com/document/465328207/wiradhany2017-pdf)
- **Multitasking in general:** a meta-analysis of 40 studies found **d = -0.624** for multitasking vs single-tasking performance. The media-multitasking subgroup effect was smaller. [MODERATE] — [PMC7614709](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7614709)
- **Task-switching costs:** switching between tasks incurs time and error costs even when switches are predictable (Rogers & Monsell 1995; Monsell 2003, Trends in Cognitive Sciences). [STRONG, lab] — [Monsell 2003 doi:10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7) (from prior knowledge)
- **Phone presence / brain drain (Ward et al. 2017, JACR):**
  - Hartmann et al. (2020, Consciousness and Cognition) did a partial replication and found **no** brain-drain effect on short-term or prospective memory. They suggested that any benefit of phone absence disappears for highly phone-dependent people (limited data).
  - Böttger, Poschik & Zierer (2023), Behavioral Sciences: meta-analysis found a significant negative overall effect, but it **pooled presence with active use**, and effects varied by cognitive domain.
  - Parry (2024), Media Psychology: six meta-analyses (k = 56, n = 7,093). Mere presence reduced **working memory capacity only**, with **null effects for other cognitive functions**, poor statistical power and methodological heterogeneity.
  - [CONTESTED] — [Böttger et al. 2023, PMC10525686](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10525686/); [BPS Research Digest on Hartmann](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/revisiting-brain-drain-effect); [VU Amsterdam (Parry 2024)](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/does-the-mere-presence-of-a-smartphone-impact-cognitive-performan/)
- **Pomodoro / fixed breaks:**
  - Biwer et al. (2023), British Journal of Educational Psychology: 87 university students during real self-study. Systematic breaks (~24 min study / 6 min break) gave **better mood and higher efficiency** (similar work in less time) than self-regulated breaks. Self-regulated students took longer sessions and longer breaks. [WEAK–MODERATE, single study, Dutch university students] — [PubMed 36859717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/); [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel)
  - A 2025 study found Pomodoro-style breaks *accelerated* fatigue and reduced motivation compared with self-regulated breaks, with no change in task completion. A 2025 scoping review concluded that time-structured breaks tended to outperform self-paced ones. [CONTESTED] — [PMC12292963](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/); [Preprints 202503.0845](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202503.0845)
- **Self-monitoring and self-regulated learning:** Zimmerman's cyclical model of self-regulated learning (forethought → performance with self-monitoring → self-reflection) underpins the post-session reflection. Monitoring that is recorded and reviewed improves goal attainment, d ≈ 0.40 (Harkin et al. 2016, above). [STRONG for monitoring generally] — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/87431); Zimmerman (2002), Theory Into Practice 41(2) [doi:10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2) (from prior knowledge)

### Inferences
- **Tab-leave tracking** is an objective measure of distraction that students can record, so it is a good self-monitoring input. Its value depends on a reflection step that turns the data into a plan, such as an if-then plan for the most frequent distraction. It also carries false-positive risk: leaving the tab to look up a formula or watch a lecture is not distraction. Let students tag interruptions ("looked something up" / "phone/social" / "break").
- Do **not** market "keep your phone in another room or lose X IQ points." The brain-drain effect is contested. Advise putting the phone out of reach on the stronger grounds of interruptions and task switching, while noting that the presence effect itself is uncertain.
- Offer Pomodoro-style timers as a **default, not a mandate**. Allow adjustable intervals (25/5, 50/10, free) and let students compare their own focus data across them, which turns the choice into an n-of-1 experiment that can feed the habit coach.
- Keep the 10-second reflection to 1–2 taps (focus rating, main distractor), with a weekly summary. Harkin's moderators favour recording plus periodic review.
- Distraction data is sensitive for teens. If parents can see it, it may feel like surveillance and invite gaming of the metric. Share aggregate trends at most, with the student's knowledge.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous RCT evidence that digital-wellbeing or focus-tracking apps improve academic outcomes.
- No India-specific data was found, for example on phone-sharing households or on studying with phone-based content, where the phone is also the study device. That is a major contextual difference for Kelvra's users.
- Exact effect sizes for Pomodoro studies, beyond the sample sizes, were not retrieved.

## Cross-cutting design implications for a web/phone study app (from the evidence above)

1. **Answer coach**
   - Run attempt → self-rate on rubric → AI criterion-level feedback (max 3 points, one "next step") → mandatory short rewrite → model answer comparison → re-attempt days later as spaced retrieval.
   - Show score bands and trends, not precise marks. Disclose that AI scores are approximate, and validate them against Indian teacher marking before claiming accuracy.
   - The AI never writes the student's answer (Bastani guardrail).
2. **Feedback tone everywhere:** use task- and process-level language. Avoid praise or criticism of the person and peer comparisons (Kluger & DeNisi: about 1/3 of feedback interventions backfire).
3. **Confidence checks:**
   - Use a 3-level tap before each answer.
   - Make confident errors visually salient with immediate correction and an early re-test (hypercorrection).
   - Show a weekly per-subject calibration chart with concrete, non-judgemental advice. Expect calibration gains before grade gains, and design extra support for low performers.
4. **Habit coach:**
   - Detect cramming and convert it into an auto-spacing plan.
   - Run one WOOP + if-then experiment per week, framed as part of a 2-month habit build.
   - Use forgiving consistency metrics instead of fragile streaks; if streaks exist, add repair and never shame a break.
   - Hold a weekly self-monitoring review.
5. **Focus tracker:**
   - Record tab-leaves with student-tagged reasons.
   - Offer configurable timers (fixed breaks as default).
   - Use a 1–2 tap reflection that feeds an if-then plan.
   - Avoid contested claims (brain drain), and treat distraction data as private to the student by default.
6. **Measurement:** effects in this literature are heterogeneous and little is tested in India. Build in A/B testing and outcome tracking (e.g., mock-test score changes) so Kelvra generates its own evidence rather than assuming effect sizes transfer.
